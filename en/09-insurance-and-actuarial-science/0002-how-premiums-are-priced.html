How Premiums Are Priced

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1

Last lesson we saw that putting many people’s risks into one pool irons individual uncertainty out into a computable expectation. This lesson answers a more practical question: how does an insurer actually price you? The answer is a plain piece of arithmetic — premium = expected payouts + costs + profit. Once you can read that sum, you can take apart the price of any policy, and you can see why the same cover costs one person less and another more.

Piece one: the pure premium — charging for the expected payouts

The largest slice of the premium has to be kept to pay future claims; this slice is the pure premium. It is a direct application of Lesson 1’s expected value: the probability of a claim, times the money paid out if the claim happens.

Pure premium = insured amount × claim probability
Worked example: insured amount ¥1,000,000, annual claim probability 0.3% → pure premium = 1,000,000 × 0.3% = ¥3,000 per year.

Check where that number comes from: suppose 10,000 people buy the policy, each with a 0.3% claim probability. The expected number of claims is 10,000 × 0.3% = 30; each claim pays ¥1,000,000, so ¥30,000,000 is needed in total; spread across 10,000 people, that is exactly ¥3,000 each. A premium is not picked out of thin air — it is simply “the whole pool’s expected claims ÷ headcount”.

The core intuition

What an insurer sells is not a promise that nothing will happen; it swaps everyone’s small certain outlays (premiums) for a few people’s large uncertain losses (claims). The pure premium is the fair price of that swap.

Piece two: the loading — costs, profit, and a safety cushion

Collect only the pure premium and the company has nothing left over: it must pay salaries and rent, advertise, pay commissions to sales channels, retain a profit, and hold a buffer for “claims run heavier than expected this year”. All of this is bundled into the loading.

Gross premium = pure premium + loading
Loading = operating costs + profit + risk reserve; in practice it is often simplified to a percentage of the pure premium. Example: at a 25% loading rate, gross premium = 3,000 × (1 + 25%) = ¥3,750 per year.
Channels and commissions, in one sentence

To judge whether a policy is expensive, look first at what it covers, then at the gross premium, and only last at “how much comes back to you” — return-of-premium products usually carry a much heavier loading, and the extra money you pay is typically just rolled at a very low return before being handed back.

Risk classification: why smokers and older applicants pay more

Why does the same product cost one person less and another more? Because the insurer is practicing risk classification: people of similar risk go into the same pool, and each group’s pure premium is computed from its own claim probability. Smokers and the elderly have markedly higher death probabilities, so their pure premiums are naturally higher — this is not discrimination but a “fair premium” that has everyone pay close to their own true risk.

Think it through in reverse: if the insurer charges everyone one price based on average risk, the healthy are subsidizing the high-risk. The healthy find it a bad deal and leave, the pool’s high-risk share rises, and premiums have to rise — that is the star of the next lesson: adverse selection. Risk classification is the first line of defense against it.

Deductibles and coinsurance: shoulder a little yourself, and the premium drops a notch

A deductible is the part you absorb yourself after a claim; coinsurance is the percentage of the remainder you share. Both cut the pure premium directly — because what you transfer to the insurer is only “the stretch you couldn’t bear”:

Pure premium after deductible = (insured amount − deductible) × claim probability
Example: with a ¥50,000 deductible, (1,000,000 − 50,000) × 0.3% = ¥2,850 per year — ¥150 saved versus no deductible.

Even better is the side effect: small losses come out of your own pocket, so the “I’m insured anyway” trips for minor ailments and minor repairs are restrained — every yuan you spend is first your own yuan. Consider this an advance preview of the next lesson’s “moral hazard”.

Don’t overshoot

Pick a deductible within the range you could pay “without flinching”. Insurance exists for risks you cannot bear; pushing the deductible up to a bone-breaking level just to save a little money puts the cart before the horse.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Break a gross premium into “pure premium + loading”, and compute for yourself that a ¥1,000,000 sum insured at 0.3% probability means a ¥3,000 pure premium;
  • Know that the loading covers operating costs, profit, and a risk reserve, and that a policy should be judged by its cover first, not by its “cash back”;
  • Understand the double duty of deductibles and coinsurance: cheaper premiums and no overuse for minor ailments;
  • See that risk classification matches premiums to risk — it is not discrimination, and it is the first line of defense against adverse selection.
