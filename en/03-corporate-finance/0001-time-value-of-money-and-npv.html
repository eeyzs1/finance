Time Value of Money and NPV: Deciding Whether a Project Is Worth It

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1 of 04 Investments recommended

¥100 today and ¥100 a year from now carry the same face value but different worth — this plain intuition is the foundation of the entire edifice of finance. This lesson teaches you to convert “future money” step by step into “today’s money”, add it all up into a single number, and so answer every investment question with the same yardstick: this project — is it worth investing in?

1. Why ¥100 today is worth more than ¥100 a year from now

time value of money sounds abstract, but everyone already understands it: if someone borrows ¥100 from you and promises to return exactly ¥100 a year later, your gut says you lose out. Where is the loss? Three reasons:

  • Opportunity cost: parked in a wealth product yielding 5% a year, that ¥100 becomes ¥105. Lend it out, and you give up that ¥5.
  • Uncertainty: whether the borrower can — or will — repay a year from now is unknown. The longer the horizon, the more that can go wrong.
  • Inflation: if prices rise 3% a year, ¥100 a year from now buys only about ¥97 of today’s goods — its purchasing power has shrunk.

Stack those three forces together and you get finance’s first axiom: money has time value, so to compare money at different points in time, you must first convert it all to the same point in time.

2. Compounding and discounting: a pair of opposite operations

Pushing money forward along time is called compounding, and what it yields is future value; pulling money back against time is called discounting, and what it yields is present value. The per-period interest rate r in the formulas has its own name — the discount rate. It is the price of “time + risk”.

FV = PV × (1 + r)n　　PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n
r is the discount rate per period and n the number of (yearly) periods. The two formulas are inverses of each other: the larger r is, and the larger n is, the harder future money gets “discounted” on its way back to today.

3. NPV: one number decides whether a project is worth it

Now that single sums can be discounted, a project is simply this: discount each year’s cash flow separately, add them up, then subtract the money paid in today. That number is the net present value (NPV):

NPV = Σ [ CFt ÷ (1 + r)t ] − initial investment　(t = 1 … n)
CFt is the net cash flow (inflows minus outflows) at the end of year t; the initial investment happens today (year 0), is already “today’s money”, and is therefore not discounted.
Decision rule

NPV > 0: invest — the project earns back more present value than it soaks up, leaving you that much wealthier today; NPV < 0: walk away — it cannot even recover the cost of the capital; NPV = 0 exactly breaks even, so investing or not makes no difference. NPV is denominated in “today’s money”, so values can be compared and summed directly.

Full worked example: invest ¥5 million, collect ¥1.5 million per year for 5 years, r = 10%

With identical cash flows every year (an annuity), you can discount the whole stream at once using the annuity factor:

Annuity factor = [ 1 − (1 + r)−n ] ÷ r
This is the sum of the year-1 through year-n discount factors, built for cash flows that are “the same amount every year”; the calculator below uses it too.

4. IRR: NPV asked a different way (and its two traps)

NPV answers “how much wealth is gained”; the internal rate of return (IRR) answers “how high is the rate of return”. One-sentence definition: the IRR is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly 0. Return to the worked example above: keep raising the discount rate and NPV falls steadily, reaching zero at about 15.2% — that is the IRR (slide the discount rate toward 15% in the calculator above and check whether the red line on the right chart sits near the zero line). Below the IRR, NPV is positive; above it, NPV is negative.

Two IRR traps

① Multiple roots: when the cash-flow sign flips midway (invest, recover, then invest again), the IRR equation can have several roots, and the one your calculator reports need not be the sensible one. ② The reinvestment assumption: IRR implicitly assumes that “cash recovered along the way can be reinvested at the IRR itself” — a 15.2% IRR does not mean you truly earn 15.2% the whole way through. So IRR is handy but not to be worshipped: whenever it conflicts with NPV, NPV always wins.

5. Real life: a lottery jackpot — take it now, or in installments?

The discount rate is the judge

After winning, there are two ways to collect: Plan A pays ¥0.9 million in a lump sum now; Plan B pays ¥0.35 million a year for 3 years (¥1.05 million in nominal total). Discount Plan B at 8%: annuity factor = [1 − 1.08⁻³] ÷ 0.08 = (1 − 0.7938) ÷ 0.08 = 2.5771, so present value = 0.35 × 2.5771 ≈ ¥0.902 million ≈ ¥0.9 million — essentially equivalent to Plan A (check: 1.08³ = 1.259712, so 1.08⁻³ = 0.7938). The judge is the discount rate: if your money can reliably earn 10%, Plan B shrinks to 0.35 × 2.4869 ≈ ¥0.87 million — take Plan A; if it can only sit in a 3% deposit, Plan B swells to 0.35 × 2.8286 ≈ ¥0.99 million — take Plan B. Same cash flows: swap the discount rate, and the verdict flips — choosing the right r matters just as much as computing the NPV correctly.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Name the three sources of the time value of money: opportunity cost, uncertainty, inflation
  • Use PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ to pull money from any date back to today, and see why larger r and larger n mean steeper discounts
  • Use NPV = Σ CFₜ ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ − initial investment to accept or reject a project, and master the annuity-factor shortcut
  • Understand the IRR’s definition (the discount rate at which NPV is zero) and its two traps; when it conflicts with NPV, NPV wins
  • Apply discounting to compare “lump sum vs. installments” choices in daily life, knowing the discount rate decides the answer
