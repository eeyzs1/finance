CAPM and the Security Market Line

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2

The last lesson closed with a hidden sentence: “the market does not pay you for risk you can diversify away.” So which risk does earn compensation — and how much? This lesson gives finance’s most famous answer — the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM): a straight line called the Security Market Line that puts a price on every share of unavoidable risk.

1. Only unavoidable risk earns compensation

Last lesson we saw that once you hold 20–30 stocks, idiosyncratic risk has mostly canceled itself out. Take the logic one step further: if a stock demanded an extra 1% of return merely because “it has a habit of accidents,” investors could simply buy a basket of stocks and diversify those accidents away — nobody is willing to pay extra for it, and competition automatically wipes out such demands.

What remains, unavoidable, is the rise and fall shared by the entire market — rate hikes, recessions, and wars moving everything up or down together. This is systematic risk. The conclusion is a single sentence: the market pays compensation only for systematic risk; how much is decided by β, the subject of the next section.

2. β: a stock’s sensitivity to the market

Beta (β) measures how sensitively a stock moves with the market portfolio:

β = 1: when the market rises 10%, the stock rises about 1×10% = 10% on average — in step. β = 1.2: a 10% market rise becomes about 1.2×10% = 12% — amplified. β = 0.8: about 0.8×10% = 8% — cushioned.

A directional impression (a memory aid only, not investment advice): “toll-collector” stocks such as utilities and large banks often carry β below 1, leaning cushioning; growth tech stocks often carry β above 1, leaning amplifying. β is estimated from historical data and shifts across periods — it is not a fixed attribute.

β is not a report card

A high or low β is not “good or bad”: β=1.2 only means more elasticity — bigger gains in bull markets, bigger losses in bear ones. In CAPM, compensation is tied to β alone; the company’s fame, or whether you happen to like it, has nothing to do with it.

3. CAPM: the line that prices risk

CAPM compresses the intuition above into a single formula:

E(Ri) = Rf + βi × ( E(Rm) − Rf )
Example: with a risk-free rate Rf = 3%, a market risk premium E(Rm) − Rf = 5%, and a stock at β = 1.2, E(Ri) = 3% + 1.2×5% = 3% + 6% = 9%. Each extra unit of β earns one more helping of the premium; at β = 0 you receive only the risk-free rate.

Plot the expected return each β “should” deliver and you get a straight line that starts at Rf with the market risk premium as its slope — the Security Market Line (SML). It is a ruler: it measures how much each share of systematic risk “ought” to sell for.

4. The market portfolio and the logic of index funds

Where does the market portfolio come from? Add up every investor’s holdings: whatever you buy extra, someone else has sold, so active positions in individual stocks cancel out, and what remains is exactly the whole market weighted by market cap. CAPM’s corollary goes one step further: that portfolio sits right on the efficient frontier of the last lesson. If the market itself is the optimal portfolio, the easiest play is to simply buy it — that is the pricing logic of passive index funds.

5. Mispricing intuition: which side of the line is the dot on?

The SML doubles as an inspection checklist. For any given β, the line states the expected return the asset “should” offer:

A dot above the line: the market’s actual expected return exceeds what it should be — same risk, higher payoff — meaning the price is too low (undervalued), a buying opportunity. A dot below the line: the actual expected return falls short — same risk, less payoff — the price is too high (overvalued), and it should be avoided or even shorted.

Check (Rf = 3%, premium 5%): a β = 1.0 stock should offer 3% + 1×5% = 8%; if the market expects 10%, the dot sits above the line → undervalued. A β = 1.5 stock should offer 3% + 1.5×5% = 3% + 7.5% = 10.5%; if the market gives only 8%, the dot sits below the line → overvalued.

Of course, if the market is efficient enough, investors will quickly trade such dots flat — proponents and skeptics of the efficient-market hypothesis have argued over this for decades. For this lesson, just keep the ruler firmly in mind; the debate can go back into the chat with your AI tutor.

Try this

① Raise Rf: the whole line shifts up, and every asset’s fair return rises together. ② Widen the premium: the line steepens, stretching out the fair returns of high-β stocks. ③ Slide β: the gold dot slides along the line — it cannot escape, because the fair return is set by β alone.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Tell the two risks apart: idiosyncratic risk is diversifiable and uncompensated; systematic risk is undiversifiable and compensated
  • Read β: equal to 1 moves in step, above 1 amplifies, below 1 cushions
  • Apply the CAPM formula: E(R) = Rf + β×premium, e.g. 3% + 1.2×5% = 9%
  • Understand the market portfolio and the logic of index funds, and use the SML to judge over- and undervaluation
