Derivatives & Financial Engineering · Lesson 3

The Intuition Behind Option Pricing

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 2 of this branch

Last lesson we drew the option’s payoff diagram at expiration, but we have not yet answered the most crucial question: what is this option actually worth today? This lesson first answers “what makes an option more expensive” with intuition, then uses a one-step binomial tree to work a complete numerical example down to the last cent — high-school math is all it takes. Finally, we take one look at what the Nobel Prize–level Black-Scholes model is really “thinking.”

The two pieces of an option’s price: recap and deepening

The conclusion of Lesson 2: premium = intrinsic value + time value. Intrinsic value can be computed at a glance; the real puzzle is time value: what makes a mere chance worth money — and how much?

The conclusion up front: the higher the volatility and the longer the time remaining, the more expensive the option. The reason is the innate asymmetry of option payoffs — the upside is uncapped, the downside has a floor (at worst the option expires worthless). Volatility can only push the underlying toward either extreme, and the option benefits from just one of them: a wild rally brings a big gain, while a hard slump still costs only the premium. The wilder the swings, the larger the expected value of the “wild rally” half — and the pricier the option.

A numerical experiment: same expectation, 4× the price

Stocks A and B, with their prices one year from now and the probabilities:

StockPessimistic scenario (50%)Optimistic scenario (50%)Expected priceExpected payoff of a strike-100 call
A (low volatility)901101000.5×0 + 0.5×10 = ¥5
B (high volatility)601401000.5×0 + 0.5×40 = ¥20

Check: for A’s call at expiration, the pessimistic state pays max(90−100,0)=0 and the optimistic state max(110−100,0)=10, so the expectation is ¥5; for B, the pessimistic state pays max(60−100,0)=0 (note: however deep the fall, the payoff is floored at 0) and the optimistic state max(140−100,0)=40, so the expectation is ¥20. The two stocks have exactly the same expected price, yet the more volatile B makes the very same option 4× as expensive. Time works the same way: the more time remains, the farther the underlying can “travel,” and the more exaggerated the extremes on both ends — while the option harvests only the upside end.

A side note on the volatility smile: plot the implied volatility of options at each strike for the same expiration, and in theory it should be a flat line, while in reality it often curves into a “smiling” U — the premium the market demands for extreme moves is greedier than the model assumes.

The one-step binomial tree: pricing to the exact cent

After intuition, some precision. The binomial option pricing model assumes: a stock trades at ¥100 today, and one year from now there are only two possibilities — a rise to 120 (up-factor u = 1.2) or a fall to 80 (d = 0.8). Find the fair price today of a call option with strike K = 100, with the risk-free rate r = 5%.

Step 1: Write down the option’s payoff in the two future states.

  • Up: the stock is 100×1.2 = 120, payoff Cup = max(120−100, 0) = ¥20;
  • Down: the stock is 100×0.8 = 80, payoff Cdown = max(80−100, 0) = ¥0.

Step 2: Compute the risk-neutral probability p. We do not forecast the true probabilities of up and down; instead we ask: which set of probabilities makes the stock’s expected growth exactly equal the risk-free rate?

p = 1 + r − du − d = 1.05 − 0.801.20 − 0.80 = 0.250.40 = 0.625
Check: p×120 + (1−p)×80 = 0.625×120 + 0.375×80 = 75 + 30 = 105 = 100×1.05 ✓ Under this set of “probabilities,” the stock is expected to grow at exactly the risk-free rate. No-arbitrage condition: d < 1+r < u must hold, otherwise p escapes [0,1], signaling that the parameters themselves contain an arbitrage opportunity.

Step 3: Use p to compute the expected payoff, then discount at the risk-free rate.

C = p×Cup + (1−p)×Cdown1 + r = 0.625×20 + 0.375×01.05 = 12.51.05 ≈ ¥11.90

Step-by-step check: expected payoff = 0.625×20 + 0.375×0 = 12.5 + 0 = ¥12.5; discounted = 12.5 ÷ 1.05 = 11.904… ≈ ¥11.90. This option should trade at roughly ¥11.90 today.

⇄ Cross-check: the replicating portfolio (a different method, the same answer)

Today, buy 0.5 shares of the stock and borrow ¥38.10 at 5%: one year later the up-state value is 0.5×120 − 38.10×1.05 = 60 − 40 = ¥20, and the down-state value is 0.5×80 − 40 = 40 − 40 = ¥0 — identical to the option’s payoffs. The replica costs 0.5×100 − 38.10 = ¥11.90 today. The two methods converge on the same number, which is exactly the power of no-arbitrage pricing: an option = a homemade mini-portfolio.

Watch closely: drag u from 1.2 to 1.5 (a wider divergence) and the option’s fair value climbs steadily from 11.90 — the arithmetic version of “volatility is the heartbeat of options.”

📈 Volatility and Option Value · The wider the divergence, the pricier the premium

With the strike fixed at 100, the risk-free rate at 5%, and the spot at 100, let the up-factor u = 1+m and the down-factor d = 1−m grow together from 0.06 to 0.5 (for smaller m, u = 1+m ≤ 1+r = 1.05, violating the no-arbitrage condition d < 1+r < u and breaking the pricing): the option’s value rises monotonically — the unlimited-upside, floored-downside asymmetry converts all of the volatility into expected payoff.

Risk-neutral probability: not a forecast, but a measuring stick

Many beginners get stuck on p: “Why use 0.625 instead of the up-probability I believe in?” The answer: p is not a forecast of the future at all, but a measuring stick. The logic of pricing is “the option is worth whatever its replica is worth” (see the cross-check above): since the option can be replicated exactly with “stock + risk-free asset,” its price depends only on this yardstick, not on how bullish or bearish you and I feel. True probabilities do of course affect the stock’s own price — but that is already reflected in today’s ¥100. This view of “calibrating the world to the risk-free rate” is called risk-neutral pricing — the most counterintuitive, and most beautiful, step in the finance of derivatives.

The Black-Scholes model: turning the tree into a continuous world

Slice the binomial tree’s “one step” ever finer — chop a year into ten thousand instants, and the two up/down states approach a continuous price path. In 1973, Black, Scholes, and Merton followed this road to a closed-form answer: the Black-Scholes model. Its idea fits in one sentence: an option can be replicated dynamically with “underlying stock + risk-free asset,” and the cost of that replication is the option’s price — precisely the continuous-time version of the Δ=0.5 shares + ¥38.10 borrowed example above. The formula contains the normal distribution function and is not required knowledge; remember the one word “replication” and you hold its soul. For the full formula, see Investopedia: Black-Scholes Model. The work won the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences and has underpinned the derivatives industry for the half-century since.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Option price = intrinsic value + time value; the bigger the swings and the longer the time, the higher the time value;
  • Asymmetry (uncapped upside, floored downside) is the root of the option’s “appetite for volatility” — at the same expected price, the option on the high-volatility stock costs 4× as much;
  • The one-step binomial tree in three moves: payoffs in the two states → p = (1+r−d)/(u−d) → expected payoff ÷ (1+r); the worked example gives ¥11.90, matching the replicating-portfolio method;
  • The soul of the Black-Scholes model is replication: an option = a homemade portfolio assembled from the underlying and a risk-free asset.
