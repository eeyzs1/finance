Insurance & Actuarial Science · Lesson 3

Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2

Lesson 2 ended on a cliffhanger: if the insurer charges everyone one price, the healthy are subsidizing the high-risk — and then what? This lesson is the heart of the entire insurance branch: whether the insurance business succeeds or fails depends almost entirely on controlling two problems born of “information asymmetry”. By the end you will understand why health insurance must be mandatory, why auto policies carry no-claim discounts, and why you should buy insurance while you are still healthy.

Information asymmetry: the insurer knows less than you do

You know your own body, your driving habits, and how sturdy your door lock is far better than any insurer does. When the two sides of a trade hold different amounts of information, that is information asymmetry. Lesson 2’s pricing formula assumed “claim probability known”; information asymmetry means the true probability is known only to you — the ground is pulled out from under the formula.

The trouble it causes falls into two kinds by timing: one before the contract is signed, called adverse selection; one after the contract is signed, called moral hazard.

Adverse selection: a mine buried before you sign

The ex-ante problem is adverse selection: those most eager to buy insurance are often the ones who know their own risk is highest. When the company prices everyone at average risk, the healthy find it expensive and quietly leave, while the high-risk find it a bargain and enthusiastically stay — the pool deteriorates under the surface while the price stands still.

The death spiral

Low-risk members leave → the high-risk share rises → per-member claim costs rise → premiums have no choice but to rise → more low-risk members leave → … This self-reinforcing loop is the death spiral.

The classic source is economist Akerlof’s 1970 “market for lemons” paper — in the used-car market, sellers know the car’s condition and buyers don’t, buyers will only pay the average price, good cars exit the market, and what remains grows ever more “lemon”. The idea earned him the 2001 Nobel Prize in Economics. The insurance market is the same story replayed.

The insurer’s three weapons

  • Mandatory enrollment: universal health coverage keeps the healthy in the pool too. Health insurance must be mandatory for economic reasons, not out of bureaucratic habit;
  • Group insurance: the whole company enrolls as one, good risks mixed with bad, so the pool is naturally stable;
  • Underwriting exams: underwriting identifies high risks before the policy is issued — rating them up, excluding conditions, or politely declining.

Moral hazard: once you’ve signed, you change

The ex-post problem is moral hazard: stop locking the door once you have theft cover? Ask for extra prescriptions once you have medical cover? When losses are no longer fully your own, behavior quietly shifts — note that most people are not deliberately defrauding the insurer; they simply stop being quite so careful.

Auto insurance’s “no-claim discount (NCD)” is a purpose-built design: consecutive claim-free years earn a discount on next year’s premium; one claim and next year’s price rises — turning “driving carefully” into something that pays cash. Its cousins are the deductible and coinsurance from Lesson 2, plus bonus-malus rates; they all share one common point: as long as you always bear part of the consequences, your behavior won’t drift.

Run it yourself: the risk-pool deterioration simulator

Setup: the pool starts with 1,000 members — 20% high-risk (expected annual claims ¥6,000) and 80% low-risk (expected annual claims ¥1,500). First-year cost per member = 0.2×6000 + 0.8×1500 = 1200 + 1200 = ¥2,400. Each later year’s premium is priced on the previous year’s per-member cost, and low-risk members drain away at the exit rate (the high-risk never leave). Click “Run 10 years” to watch the premium spiral upward at the default 8% exit rate; then tick mandatory enrollment and watch the curve flatten on the spot.

Push the exit rate above 20% and watch the spiral accelerate: the premium curve grows ever steeper, and within ten years the pool is dominated by the high-risk. That is why a single-price pool of voluntary buyers rarely survives for long — either classify and price by risk, make enrollment mandatory, or accept the spiral.

What this means for you personally

Two golden rules

① Buy insurance while you are healthy: wait until a check-up finds something, and underwriting may decline you, rate you up, or exclude the condition — insurance is the one product that is easiest to buy when you need it least. ② Insure only the “unbearable” risks: small risks you can absorb are cheaper to retain behind a deductible, so concentrate your premium budget on the losses that would truly break through your finances.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Tell adverse selection from moral hazard in one sentence: hidden information before signing versus hidden action after;
  • Watch the death spiral unfold with your own runs: the pool worsens → prices rise → more people leave → it worsens further;
  • Read the insurer’s arsenal: mandatory enrollment, group insurance, underwriting exams, plus deductibles, coinsurance, and bonus-malus rates (NCD);
  • Remember the personal lesson: insure early while healthy, and hand insurance only the risks you could not bear.
