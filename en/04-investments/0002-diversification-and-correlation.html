Diversification and Correlation

About 18 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1

In Lesson 1 we learned to score assets with expected return and standard deviation. This lesson puts two assets into the same basket: you will find that return is just a simple weighted average, while risk can be “discounted” — and the size of the discount depends on a number called the correlation coefficient. Once you understand it, you understand why diversification is called the only free lunch in finance.

1. The portfolio’s expected return: an honest weighted average

Put a fraction w of your money into asset A and (1−w) into asset B, and the portfolio’s expected return holds no surprises:

μp = w × μA + (1 − w) × μB
Example: if A’s expected return is μA = 8% and B’s is μB = 4%, with 50% invested in each, then μp = 0.5×8% + 0.5×4% = 4% + 2% = 6%. The expected return is always the weighted average of the parts — putting the assets together neither adds to it nor takes away from it.

2. The portfolio’s risk: not a weighted average

Risk is computed entirely differently. The portfolio standard deviation formula is:

σp = √( w²σA² + (1−w)²σB² + 2·w·(1−w)·ρ·σA·σB )
Intuition: under the square root, the first two terms are the risks each asset contributes on its own; the third term is the key — it carries the correlation coefficient ρ on its back. When ρ is negative, the whole third term turns into a “subtraction” and shaves a chunk straight off the total risk; the closer ρ gets to −1, the deeper the cut.

If risk were also a weighted average like return, then 50% A (σA=15%) plus 50% B (σB=5%) would carry 0.5×15% + 0.5×5% = 10% risk. But as long as ρ is below +1, the formula above is guaranteed to come out lower than 10%. That saved slice is diversification’s gift.

3. The correlation coefficient ρ: diversification’s master knob

ρ ranges from −1 to +1, and it sets the size of the discount. Stay with the example of 50% each in A (σA=15%) and B (σB=5%) and consider three typical cases:

ρ = +1: perfectly in step

The two assets move like conjoined twins, and the third term is added in full: σp is exactly the weighted average, 10%. Buying two assets that “move the same way” diversifies nothing — a wasted effort.

ρ = 0: unrelated

The third term vanishes entirely: σp = √(0.25×0.0225 + 0.25×0.0025) = √0.00625 ≈ 7.9%. Merely being “uncorrelated” already puts you 2.1 percentage points below 10%.

ρ = −1: a perfect hedge

When A rises, B falls by exactly the right proportion, and the third term becomes a subtraction: σp = √(0.005625 + 0.000625 − 0.00375) = √0.0025 = 5%. Set the weights to 25% A + 75% B (15%×0.25 = 5%×0.75) and you can even squeeze the risk down to 0.

Real stocks can rarely hedge perfectly: within the same market, most pairs of stocks show ρ between 0.2 and 0.4. The good news — that is already enough to cut risk substantially (we run the numbers with 0.3 below).

The only free lunch

In the example above, the portfolio’s expected return stays at 6% the whole time — not a cent less; yet the risk drops from the 10% weighted average to 7.9% (ρ=0) or even 5% (ρ=−1). Lower risk without sacrificing any expected return — which is why diversification is called the only free lunch in finance. What you pay is not return; it is simply the discipline of not staking everything on a single asset.

4. The efficient frontier: tuning the weights to the optimum

Turn the weight w slowly from 0 to 1: each w drops one point (σp, μp) on the risk–return plane, and all the points together trace out a bullet-shaped curve. The upper segment of the curve — the portfolios with the highest return at each level of risk — is called the efficient frontier. Rational investors pick their portfolio only along this stretch.

The other approach is “adding names”: surprises unique to a single stock (a failed product, a lawsuit, trouble in the executive suite) are called idiosyncratic risk, and the more names you hold, the more cleanly they cancel one another out. As a rule of thumb, 20–30 stocks already diversify away most idiosyncratic risk; beyond that, each added name delivers diminishing risk reduction. An index fund buys hundreds of stocks at once — outsourcing this chore to a basket.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Compute a portfolio’s expected return as a weighted average, and know that portfolio risk is not a weighted average
  • Read the formula’s third term, 2w(1−w)ρσAσB: when ρ is negative it becomes a subtraction, and that is where the discount comes from
  • Remember the three cases: ρ=+1 diversifies nothing, ρ=0 already earns a discount, ρ=−1 hedges perfectly
  • Understand the efficient frontier and the “20–30 stocks are enough” rule of thumb, and why index funds exist
