Money & Banking · Lesson 3

Central Banks and Monetary Policy

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2 · Fractional Reserve Banking and Money Creation

Commercial banks can create deposit money, but the master switch of the whole game sits in the central bank's hands. This lesson introduces the central bank's three functions and three policy tools, then tracks how one rate cut travels through six links, step by step, down to the monthly mortgage payment you and I make.

1. The Central Bank: The Bankers' Bank

A central bank does not do business with you; it serves banks and the system as a whole. Its three functions:

1. A monopoly on issuing currency

Banknotes are issued exclusively by the central bank. This is the ultimate source of money's credit, and the foundation of all its influence.

2. The bankers' bank

Commercial banks park their reserves in accounts at the central bank and settle interbank payments across its books; more important, the central bank acts as the lender of last resort — when a bank suffers a run and no peer dares lend to it, the central bank steps in with a backstop loan, keeping panic from toppling the whole system like dominoes (the Federal Reserve played this role at scale during the 2008 financial crisis).

3. Safeguarding financial stability

It formulates and carries out monetary policy, watching over prices, employment, and the stability of the financial system — cooling things down in a boom and propping things up in a downturn.

3. The Transmission Chain: A Rate Cut's Six-Step Journey

Once a tool is engaged, the effect does not appear in an instant; it travels along a chain, link by link:

① The central bank cuts the policy rate → ② interbank rates fall → ③ deposit and loan rates come down → ④ borrowing costs fall for firms and households → ⑤ investment and consumption recover → ⑥ prices and employment improve

If any link jams (say, banks are afraid to lend or firms unwilling to borrow), the transmission fades — which is why central banks keep stressing the need to “unclog the transmission mechanism.” The animation below lights up the six steps for you.

4. Directional Effects: Rate Cuts and Hikes

Directional effects (usually in this direction, never guaranteed)

Rate cut: borrowing gets cheaper → credit expands and investment and consumption warm up — usually good for stocks and bonds (the discount rate on future cash flows falls), and mortgage payments decline. A hike works the other way: borrowing gets dearer → credit tightens, usually weighing on stock and bond prices, and payments rise. Note that this is only the “usual direction” — actual outcomes also depend on market confidence, expectations, and the external environment; a rate cut does not necessarily lift the stock market right away.

One step further: the debate over central bank independence

Should monetary policy answer to the government? Supporters of independence argue that an independent central bank can resist the political temptation to “print money for growth” and guard the currency's stability; critics counter that a central bank is untouched by elections yet deeply affects millions of households, so someone must hold it accountable. Institutional arrangements differ across countries, and this debate still has no final verdict.

5. Running the Numbers: How a Cut Lands on Your Mortgage

Take a ¥1,000,000, 30-year (360-installment) mortgage with equal monthly installments. The payment formula:

M = P · r(1+r)ⁿ ÷ ((1+r)ⁿ − 1), where r = annual rate ÷ 12 and n = 360
P is the ¥1,000,000 loan principal, r the monthly rate, n the number of installments. Check: at an annual rate of 4.9%, M ≈ ¥5,307; at 4.65%, M ≈ ¥5,156; at 4.4%, M ≈ ¥5,008.
