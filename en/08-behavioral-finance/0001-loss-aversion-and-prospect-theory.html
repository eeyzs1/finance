Loss Aversion and Prospect Theory: Why Losing ¥100 Hurts So Much

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Branch 04 Investments recommended

The same ¥100: the joy of finding it and the sting of losing it are worlds apart in intensity. In 1979, two psychologists — Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky — used a handful of choice problems a schoolchild could solve to overturn finance's “rational man” assumption, directly giving birth to behavioral finance (Kahneman received the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for this work). In this lesson you will take their original choice problems yourself, drag a slider to see your own “pain multiple,” and get an early view of the psychological machinery behind most retail investors' inability to hold winners and cut losers.

1. 1979: A Revolution Against the “Rational Man”

Traditional finance's grand edifice — from portfolio theory to option pricing — rests on a single foundation stone: expected utility theory. It assumes people are rational: they choose according to the expected utility of final wealth, multiply probabilities by outcomes, add up the weighted results, and pick whichever option has the higher expected value. The model is elegant, but it has one fatal flaw: real people do not behave this way.

In 1979, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky published “Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk” in Econometrica, a top economics journal. With a series of classroom choice problems they showed that human deviations from rationality are not random noise but systematic and predictable — directional, patterned, and reproducible. Tversky died young in 1996; Kahneman received the 2002 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences for this research (the prize is not awarded posthumously).

The one-sentence version

People do not decide around “how much money I end up with” but around “whether I'm up or down relative to some baseline” — and they are especially allergic to “down.” That is prospect theory.

2. First, the Problems: Two Classic Choices

Gains frame: take 3000 for certain, or gamble for 4000?

A: Receive ¥3000 for certain. B: An 80% chance of receiving ¥4000, and a 20% chance of receiving nothing.

First compute the expected values: B = 0.8 × 4000 = ¥3200, which is ¥200 more than A. The “rational man” assumption says pick B, yet in Kahneman and Tversky's experiment, about 80% of participants chose the certain ¥3000 (original paper, Problem 2; 95 participants). People systematically overweight the pull of “100% certain,” giving up a higher expected value for it — this is the certainty effect.

Losses frame: change one sign, and all the answers flip

C: Lose ¥3000 for certain. D: An 80% chance of losing ¥4000, and a 20% chance of losing nothing.

D's expected value = 0.8 × (−4000) = −¥3200, worse than C. “Rationality” says pick C and lose less, but this time about 92% of participants chose D (original paper, Problem 2′; 95 participants): facing losses, people switch from playing it safe to gambling, hoping to “take a shot at losing nothing.” The same person flips risk attitudes 180 degrees the moment the sign changes — expected utility theory cannot explain this; prospect theory can.

3. The Three Ingredients of Prospect Theory

1. Reference point: you evaluate the gap, not the wealth

Prospect theory's first axiom: people measure gains and losses relative to a reference point, not relative to final wealth. Example: you earn ¥200,000 a year, but before joining the company you expected ¥250,000 — under prospect theory your subjective experience is “down ¥50,000,” even though objectively you are doing well. The reference point can be an expectation, a purchase price, or a neighbor's returns; move it, and the same money feels completely different. This explains why “making money yet still unhappy” is so common.

2. Loss aversion: pain beats pleasure

The second axiom: loss aversion. For the same ¥100, the “pain” of losing it is roughly 2–2.5 times the “pleasure” of gaining it. In their 1992 follow-up study, Tversky and Kahneman estimated the loss-aversion coefficient λ ≈ 2.25. It explains a whole string of phenomena: why many people cannot bear to open a losing account, why stopping losses is so hard, and why the obsession with “selling once I break even” is so strong.

3. Probability weighting: the brain is not a calculator

The third axiom: people's “psychological weighting” of probabilities does not equal the objective probability — very small probabilities are markedly overweighted, while near-certain ones are underweighted. That is why events with probabilities like 5% or 0.1% feel far more frequent than they really are. Two businesses thrive on this at once: lotteries (a tiny chance of a big prize — in the original paper's Problem 7, 72% of participants chose a 0.1% chance of winning ¥5000 over a certain ¥5) and insurance (a 0.1% chance of losing ¥5000 vs. paying a ¥5 premium for certain — 92% chose the latter) — one and the same psychological bias underpins markets in both directions.

4. The S-Shaped Value Function: Three Ingredients, One Curve

v(x) = x0.88 (x ≥ 0)　　v(x) = −λ·(−x)0.88 (x < 0), λ ≈ 2.25
x is the gain or loss relative to the reference point (in ¥); the exponent 0.88 makes the gain segment concave and the loss segment convex (the estimate in Tversky & Kahneman, 1992); λ is the loss-aversion coefficient, which sets how much steeper the loss segment is than the gain segment.

This curve has three features, and each one maps to an investing behavior:

① The gain segment is concave: after already gaining ¥50, another ¥50 adds less pleasure — the mathematical root of “quitting while ahead.” ② The loss segment is convex: after already losing ¥50, another ¥50 adds less pain — the mathematical root of “I'm down anyway, might as well wait.” ③ The loss segment is steeper: its slope is about λ ≈ 2.25 times that of the gain segment — the pain of losing ¥100 takes roughly ¥225 of gain to offset.

A three-step way to read the chart

Step 1, look at the curvature: the green segment bends to the right (diminishing marginal pleasure), the red segment bends downward (diminishing marginal pain); step 2, look at the steepness: how much steeper red is than green is your λ; step 3, drag the slider: set λ to 1 and the two segments become perfectly symmetric — the rational-man world with no pain asymmetry.

5. The Financial Implication: A Preview of the Disposition Effect

Bring the value function into the stock market and the answer is plain. Selling a winning stock = “banking the profit” on the concave gain segment — psychologically, cashing in pleasure; selling a losing stock = turning the “paper loss” on the convex loss segment into a stamped and confirmed pain. So most retail investors systematically fail to hold winners and fail to cut losers — this is the disposition effect. Odean (1998) analyzed roughly 10,000 retail accounts at a large brokerage: investors were about 1.5 times as inclined to sell a winning stock as a losing one.

Knowing a bias ≠ immunity to it

Experimental research repeatedly finds that merely “knowing” a bias exists only weakens it slightly; it does not remove it. So the defense against bias is not willpower but rules written down in advance — for example, “when you buy, write down the stop-loss price and the rebalancing date at the same time.” Lesson 2, A Field Guide to Cognitive Biases, provides the full bias checklist and the matching rules.

Carry this question into the next lesson: if everyone is biased, why are you so often the one losing while the market stays “mostly right”? The answer: individual biases cancel one another out, but under certain conditions they also aggregate into a same-direction torrent — that is the bubble story of Lesson 3.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Compute expected values, and explain why “higher expected value ≠ what most people choose” (0.8×4000=3200 still loses to a certain 3000)
  • State the three ingredients of prospect theory: reference point, loss aversion (λ≈2.25), probability weighting (overweighting small probabilities)
  • Read the S-shaped value function: concave gains → quit while ahead; convex losses → hold on; steeper loss segment → losses hurt more
  • Understand how it previews the disposition effect — “can't hold winners, can't cut losers” — with the antidote coming in the next lesson
