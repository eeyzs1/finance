Option Payoff Structures

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 1 of this branch

A futures contract is a promise you must keep; an option swaps the “must” for a “may” — you pay a sum to buy the right to buy or sell at an agreed price in the future, and if the market turns against you, you can simply walk away. This lesson teaches you to draw the payoff diagram at expiration for any option position: it is the lingua franca of the derivatives world, and once you can read it, every “fancy strategy” resolves into a collage of a few bent lines.

Options: paying for a “may, not must”

An option is a contract: after the buyer pays the premium, it gains the right — but not the obligation to buy or sell the underlying at the strike price on the expiration date; the seller pockets the premium and assumes the obligation to honor the buyer’s exercise whenever the buyer chooses.

Two directions and two roles combine into four basic positions:

  • call option: bets on the underlying rising. The buyer has the right to buy at K — exercise if it rises, walk away if it falls;
  • put option: bets on the underlying falling. The buyer has the right to sell at K — exercise if it falls, walk away if it rises.

Remember one maxim: the premium is the buyer’s maximum loss and the seller’s maximum gain. The buyer can lose no more than the premium; the seller can earn no more than the premium — but in exchange the seller shoulders an obligation that may be bottomlessly deep.

The four basic positions: one table to see them all

Let ST be the underlying’s price on the expiration date, K the strike price, and P the premium. Payoffs at expiration for the four positions (per share / per unit of the underlying):

Three rules: ① The seller’s payoff = the buyer’s payoff × (−1) — mirror images; ② every bent line kinks at ST = K; ③ break-even point = strike price ± premium (+ for calls, − for puts).

Break-even point: strike price ± premium

Check one example: strike price K = ¥100, premium P = ¥8.

  • Long call: at an expiration price of ¥108, exercising earns 108−100 = ¥8, exactly offsetting the ¥8 premium — break-even point = 100 + 8 = ¥108; at an expiration price of 120 the gain is 120−100−8 = ¥12; at 92 (<100) the buyer lets the option lapse, capping the loss at ¥8.
  • Long put: at an expiration price of ¥92, exercising earns 100−92 = ¥8, exactly offsetting the premium — break-even point = 100 − 8 = ¥92; if the underlying falls to 0, the gain is at its maximum = 100−0−8 = ¥92.

Intuitive reading: the break-even point is the price at which the market has earned your premium back for you. The pricier the premium, the farther the market must travel before you break even.

Intrinsic value and time value: the two pieces of an option’s price

Options trade before their expiration date, and the market price splits into two pieces:

Premium P = intrinsic value + time value

Example: a stock trades at ¥105. A call with strike 100 is quoted at ¥9 — exercising immediately earns 105−100 = ¥5 (intrinsic value 5), and the other ¥4 buys the chance that it may keep rising before expiration (time value 4). A call with strike 120 is quoted at ¥3 — intrinsic value 0 (no one exercises at a loss), so all ¥3 is time value. Why that “chance” is worth money — and how much — is exactly the subject of Lesson 3.

Putting them together: buying “insurance” and collecting “rent” on your stock

An option’s greatest power lies in combining it with a spot position. The two classics:

Protective put: hold the stock and buy a put alongside it — the equivalent of buying insurance for the stock. Suppose the shares were bought at ¥100 and you buy a put with strike 90 and premium 8: if the share price crashes to 0, the stock loses 100 while the put earns 90−8 = 82, for a total loss of only ¥18. Step-by-step check: maximum loss = (cost of the shares − strike price) + premium = (100−90) + 8 = ¥18; the price is a break-even point raised to 100 + 8 = ¥108. The strike at 90 works like the “deductible” in an insurance policy — it covers crashes below 90, while the smaller damage between 90 and 100 is yours to bear.

Covered call: hold the stock and sell a call against it — the equivalent of collecting rent on the stock. The premium lands in your pocket and boosts returns; the cost is that if the share price soars, your stock gets “called away” at the strike price, capping your upside. It suits holders who do not plan to sell and do not expect much near-term movement. The two combinations — one defensive, one offensive — are the option strategies institutions and individuals use most.

✓ Key takeaways
  • An option = a right, not an obligation; the premium is the buyer’s maximum loss and the seller’s maximum gain;
  • Payoff diagrams of the four basic positions: long call (loss limited, gain unlimited), short call (mirror image), long put (a bent line with slope 0/−1), short put (mirror image) — all kinked at K;
  • Break-even point = strike price ± premium (with K=100 and P=8: 108 for a call, 92 for a put);
  • Premium = intrinsic value + time value; the protective put installs a “floor” under the stock, the covered call collects “rent” on it.
