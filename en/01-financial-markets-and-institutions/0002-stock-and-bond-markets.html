The two most basic securities: stock and bond markets

About 18 minutes Difficulty: Beginner Prerequisites: Lesson 1 (the two roads for funds)

In Lesson 1, what travels along the direct-finance channel is “securities.” The two oldest members of the securities family are stocks and bonds: one sells ownership in the company, the other sells a claim on the company as a creditor. This lesson first separates the two stages — the primary market, where money reaches the company for the first time, and the secondary market, where investors trade among themselves. Then it runs an experiment: one company, the same ¥100 million raised — where exactly do the real costs of issuing bonds and of issuing new shares diverge?

1. The primary market: money reaches the issuer for the first time

The primary market is the securities’ “assembly workshop.” A company selling shares to the public for the first time is doing an initial public offering (IPO); a firm or government selling bonds for the first time is said to issue bonds.

To judge whether a transaction belongs to the primary market, there is only one question: is the money flowing into the issuer’s account for the first time? When you subscribe to newly issued IPO shares, the funds enter the listed company and pay for factories, R&D, or repaying old debt — that is the primary market. The company is usually accompanied through the offering by an investment bank (one of the stars of Lesson 3), which collects an underwriting commission but keeps none of the proceeds.

2. The secondary market: investors trading among themselves

The secondary market is the securities’ “secondhand circulation market.” When you buy a round lot of an already-listed stock at the market price in your trading app, the seller is another investor on the way out — not one cent of that money enters the company’s account.

So what is the secondary market for?

At least three things. First, it provides an exit — precisely because you can sell at any time, you dare commit money in the primary market to a project that runs five or ten years. Second, continuous pricing — secondary-market prices are the reference point for pricing the company’s next share or bond issue. Third, discipline on management — a persistently falling share price transmits pressure. Without secondary-market liquidity, primary-market issuance would dry up at once: no one wants to buy an asset with “no way out.” That property is called liquidity.

3. Stocks: ownership and the residual claim

Buy a stock and you become a shareholder of the company, owning two things: voting rights (a say in major decisions proportional to your shares) and a residual claim.

The “residual” means company revenue must first pay wages, supplier invoices, interest, and taxes; whatever profit is left is shared by the shareholders — it may be a fortune, or nothing at all. So a stock’s payoff structure is unlimited on the upside, zero on the downside: if the company grows big, profits and valuation can multiply tenfold; if it goes under, you lose at most the principal you put in (that is “limited liability”) and never owe more. The price you pay: you get your money last.

4. Bonds: debt claims and fixed cash flows

Buy a bond and you are a creditor of the company — in essence, a lender collecting interest. The contract is strict: interest is paid at fixed intervals at the coupon rate, and the principal is repaid at maturity. The payoff structure is capped on the upside, default on the downside: if the company’s profits multiply tenfold, you still receive only the agreed coupon; if the business fails, you may not even get your principal back. That is default risk.

But creditors hold one privilege shareholders do not: priority in repayment. When a company is liquidated in bankruptcy, the proceeds from selling assets first cover liquidation costs, employee wages, and taxes; then come the bondholders; shareholders are last — in most bankruptcies they recover almost nothing. “A fixed amount, but first in line” versus “the residual, but last in line” is the whole difference between a bond and a share of stock.

5. An experiment: one company, bonds or new shares?

Enough theory. Set up one company: it needs ¥100 million for a new project, and two plans sit on the table —

  • Plan A · Issue bonds: issue ¥100 million of bonds at a 5% coupon, with ¥5 million of interest every year, in good years and bad alike;
  • Plan B · Issue new shares: issue 10 million new shares at ¥10 each to raise ¥100 million; the new shareholders end up with 10% of the total (total shares become 100 million, with existing shareholders holding 90 million).

Assumptions: pre-interest profit is the same under both plans; the ¥5 million of annual bond interest is paid before anything is distributed to shareholders; the share-issue plan pays no interest, but all profit is shared by 100 million shares, of which existing shareholders hold 90%; taxes and issuance costs are ignored. The break-even point sits at ¥50 million of pre-interest profit — exactly ¥100 million × 5%, that is, “the return on the new money equals the coupon rate.” Drag the slider until profit reaches ¥50 million and watch.

Take the experiment’s conclusion with you

Interest is a fixed expense; an equity share is a proportional expense. In good years (pre-interest profit > ¥50 million), paying fixed interest is cheaper than giving up 10% of profit, so bonds let existing shareholders earn more. In bad years (< ¥50 million), the proportional expense shrinks automatically, and the share issue actually protects existing shareholders’ returns — once profit falls below ¥5 million, the bond plan even drags them straight into a loss. This is the first concrete example of “debt amplifies returns and amplifies risk”; Branch 03 (Corporate Finance) will develop it into a full theory of leverage.

6. Exchanges and over-the-counter markets

The secondary market, too, comes in two forms. A stock exchange (Shanghai Stock Exchange, Shenzhen Stock Exchange, New York Stock Exchange) is a centralized matching venue: standardized contracts, public quotes, matched trades, central clearing — everyone sees the same price.

The other form is the over-the-counter (OTC) market: there is no unified trading floor; securities dealers act as market makers quoting both a buy and a sell price, trading one-on-one with customers. China’s interbank bond market (the main arena for government and financial bonds) and Nasdaq in its early American years are both OTC. Generally speaking, exchange markets offer high transparency and strong liquidity; OTC markets are flexible and can accommodate non-standardized products, but price transparency is lower.

7. How liquidity moves prices: the bid-ask spread

Liquidity sounds abstract, but in fact it is written on the quote screen every second of the day: the bid-ask spread. If you want to buy immediately, you must accept the best ask; if you want to sell immediately, you must accept the best bid — the spread is the service fee you pay for “right now.”

Spread percentage = (ask − bid) ÷ mid-price × 100%
Mid-price = (bid + ask) ÷ 2. Compare the two stocks below: one round trip on the popular stock costs about 0.012% in hidden costs, versus about 1.40% on the obscure one — a gap of more than a hundredfold. On the same ¥100,000, merely “walking in and out” of the obscure stock grinds away roughly ¥1,400 to the spread.

Popular large-cap stock ¥1700.00 ¥1700.20 ¥0.20 ≈ 0.012% Crowds queuing to buy and sell; market-making risk is small, so the spread is squeezed to nearly nothing
Obscure small-cap stock ¥8.50 ¥8.62 ¥0.12 ≈ 1.40% Buyers and sellers are scarce; quote dealers need a wide spread to protect themselves, leaving investors with high hidden costs

This explains an important phenomenon: liquidity itself moves prices. The worse the liquidity, the bigger the discount buyers demand and the deeper the discount sellers are forced to accept — for the same asset, “how easily it sells” directly changes “what it is worth.” In extreme markets a liquidity spiral can even appear: prices fall → would-be sellers multiply while buyers vanish → spreads blow out → prices fall further. Keep that chain in mind; Branch 06 (Risk Management) revisits it under liquidity risk.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Separate the two markets with one question — does the money enter the issuer’s account for the first time? If it does, primary market; if it only changes hands between investors, secondary market;
  • You can state every difference between stocks and bonds: ownership vs debt claim, residual but unlimited vs fixed but capped, repaid last vs first in line;
  • You verified by hand that “interest is a fixed expense, equity dilution a proportional one”: ¥50 million of pre-interest profit is the break-even point for ¥100 million raised at a 5% coupon;
  • You can read a bid-ask spread: the gap between 0.012% and 1.40% is the price of liquidity, and you understand why illiquid assets must trade at a discount.
