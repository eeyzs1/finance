International Finance · Lesson 2

What Determines Exchange Rates

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 1

Last lesson we learned to “read” an exchange rate: appreciation and depreciation, bid and ask prices. This lesson answers the more fundamental question: what determines it? The answer depends on how far ahead you look — in the short run, the exchange rate is an asset price pushed around by flows of money; in the long run, it eventually reverts to the purchasing power of goods and services.

The framework in one sentence

In the short run, watch capital flows and interest rate differentials; in the long run, the rate gravitates back to purchasing power. Remember this sentence and every exchange-rate story slots into the right box: a central bank's rate decision is short-run news; a country's inflation trend is long-run news.

1. Purchasing Power Parity: The Long-Run Anchor

Imagine an identical good circulating freely around the world: arbitrageurs would flatten every price gap — if it is expensive in country A and cheap in country B, merchants buy in B, ship to A, and sell there, until the prices agree once converted into the same currency. This is the law of one price.

Stretch “one good” into “one basket of goods” and you get purchasing power parity (PPP): the long-run sensible level of the exchange rate between two countries is roughly the ratio of their price levels. A currency with higher inflation loses purchasing power faster and tends to depreciate over the long run.

How can we test it? Since 1986 The Economist has used a clever (and cheeky) “standard commodity basket”: the Big Mac Index. The Big Mac's recipe is nearly identical worldwide, yet it bundles local rent, labor, and ingredients — a miniature “price basket”. Drag the slider below and run the numbers yourself:

Interactive · Big Mac Index drill
Implied PPP rate: 4.47 RMB per USD Gap vs. actual 7.2: +38% Verdict: purchasing power undervalued

Check the default case: a US Big Mac costs about US$5.7 and a Chinese one ¥25.5, so the implied PPP rate = 25.5 ÷ 5.7 ≈ 4.47 RMB per USD, while the actual rate is about 7.2. The implied value is (7.2 − 4.47) ÷ 7.2 ≈ 38% below the actual one — hence that famous, crude inference: renminbi purchasing power is “undervalued”. For the implied rate to catch up with 7.2, a Chinese Big Mac would have to sell at 5.7 × 7.2 ≈ ¥41 — far beyond the slider's range, which should already make you suspicious.

The Big Mac Index's limitations

It is a teaching aid, not a trading signal: ① rent, labor, and other non-tradables make up most of a Big Mac's cost and cannot be arbitraged across borders; ② tastes and competitive intensity differ across countries, so pricing strategies differ; ③ taxes and fees differ widely. Professional PPP estimates use hundreds or even thousands of goods, and their conclusions still deviate noticeably from actual rates — take “undervalued by 38%” with a big discount.

2. Interest Rate Parity: The Short-to-Medium-Run Anchor

Money instinctively chases interest rate differentials. If the dollar yields 5% and the renminbi 2%, borrow renminbi, convert into dollars, and “eat the interest” — a free 3 percentage points a year? There is no such free lunch: the way you lock away exchange-rate risk today is to sign, at the same time, a contract to convert back at the forward rate, and the market closes the arbitrage gap through the forward premium or discount.

Interest rate parity (IRP) is precisely about this: the high-interest currency trades at a forward discount, the low-interest currency at a forward premium. Covered arbitrageurs collect the interest differential while locking the forward — as long as the two are out of line, someone steps in, until the profit falls to zero.

Forward rate F = spot rate S × (1 + rquote currency) ÷ (1 + rbase currency)
For USD/CNY (how many RMB one US dollar is worth): the base currency is the US dollar, the quote currency the renminbi. Once the forward is priced this way, covered arbitrage becomes unprofitable — the extra yield from holding the high-interest dollar is exactly eaten by the forward discount.

Plug in numbers: spot USD/CNY = 7.2, dollar rate 5%, renminbi rate 2%:

F = 7.2 × (1 + 2%) ÷ (1 + 5%) = 7.344 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 6.99. The forward price is below the spot 7.2 — the dollar is at a forward discount: locking in a dollar one year from today buys fewer renminbi. The 3 percentage points of extra interest you ate are handed straight back through the discount — the exchange-rate version of “there is no free lunch”.

Fit the two parities onto one timetable

PPP governs the long run (years): the ratio of price levels draws the exchange rate's center of gravity; IRP governs the short-to-medium run (months to a year): interest differentials set the forward and the destination of carry money. The day-to-day bouncing around in the news comes mostly from the “capital flows and expectations” of the next section.

3. Four Factors, One Map

Picture the exchange rate as a balance scale, with the pull of the two currencies pressing on the pans. The main ballast weights are four:

FactorHow it worksTime horizonExample in one sentence
Interest rate differentialA high interest rate attracts cross-border inflows, lifting the currency in the short runShort termThe Fed hikes aggressively; the dollar index strengthens
Inflation differentialA currency with higher inflation loses purchasing power faster and tends to depreciateLong termThe Turkish lira and Argentine peso in high-inflation years
Growth and tradeFast-growing economies with big surpluses see stronger demand for their currencyMedium to long termRenminbi appreciation pressure at export peaks
Capital flows and expectationsExpectations fulfill themselves: money floods toward currencies “sure to rise”, then stampedes the other wayShort and violentThe dollar and yen when risk aversion heats up

The four weights often fight one another: rate differentials push the dollar up, twin deficits push it down, and the winner is whichever story the market currently believes. Hence “in the short run the exchange rate is a voting machine; in the long run, a weighing machine”.
