Risk Management · Lesson 1

The Risk Map and VaR: “How Much Can I Lose?” in One Sentence

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 1 of 04 Investments recommended

Return answers “how much can I make”; risk answers “how much can I lose”. This lesson first sorts the bewildering variety of risks into four great families and learns how each of them strikes; then it teaches VaR, the measuring language institutions use worldwide — one sentence that says “under normal conditions, this much at most” — and also shows you where that sentence falls short.

1. The Risk Families: Know Your Enemy First

With any institutional report in hand, step one is to slot each risk into its pigeonhole. Financial risks fall mainly into four types; each has its own modus operandi, and each calls for a different remedy.

Risk typeMechanism in one sentenceClassic case
market riskPrices move, P&L appears instantly: swings in stock, interest-rate, FX, and commodity prices change asset value directly.In China’s 2015 A-share crash, over a thousand stocks hit limit-down day after day; the heavier your position, the harder you fell.
credit riskA counterparty defaults: the promised repayment of principal and interest doesn’t arrive on schedule, and the value of the claim shrinks on the spot.Property developers defaulted on their dollar bonds; the bonds crashed and holders took the loss.
operational riskThe enemy within: rogue employees, process loopholes, system failures — losses come from internal control breakdowns, not from the market.In 1995, Nick Leeson’s unauthorized derivatives trades brought down Barings Bank, a 233-year-old institution.
liquidity riskCan’t sell when you need to: buyers vanish in an instant, so you must accept a deep discount — or cannot trade at all.In the March 2020 panic, even U.S. Treasuries — the safest asset there is — saw their liquidity evaporate.
Risks travel in chains

The four types often erupt together: Barings’ direct losses came from market positions (market risk), but the root cause that let losses run wild was failed internal control (operational risk); a developer default (credit risk) often arrives alongside its bonds becoming unsellable (liquidity risk). Find the primary cause first, then trace the chain reaction.

2. VaR: Three Elements, None Optional

With the taxonomy done, the next question is “how big is it”. Institutions’ most common answer is Value at Risk (VaR): it compresses the risk of an investment or a portfolio into a single sentence. A proper VaR statement must contain all three elements at once:

  • Amount: how large a loss (e.g. ¥1 million);
  • Horizon: the loss over what period (e.g. one trading day);
  • Confidence level: with what probability (e.g. 95%).
95% confidence level · 1 trading day · VaR = ¥1 million
How to read it: under normal market conditions, on about 95% of trading days the loss will not exceed ¥1 million; on average about 1 trading day in 20 will exceed it (1 ÷ (1 − 95%) = 20).

Confidence level and “once every N days” are reciprocals: 90% means once every 10 trading days, 95% once every 20, 99% once every 100. The higher the confidence level, the larger VaR must be — the more certainty you demand, the deeper the “safety line” has to be drawn.

A VaR quoted as a bare number is meaningless

“Our VaR is ¥1 million” — is that one day or one year? 95% confidence or 99%? The meanings are worlds apart. When you see an amount quoted with no horizon or confidence level, demand the other two elements before deciding whether to believe it.

3. VaR’s Two Blind Spots

Misreading warning

VaR is not the maximum loss: it only says “on about 95% of days you lose less than this” — on the remaining 5% of days you can still lose far more. Nor does VaR tell you how much you lose once it is exceeded — that is its “tail blind spot”. Treating VaR as a loss ceiling is the classic opening move of countless risk-management disasters.

The standard fix for the tail blind spot is ES (Expected Shortfall): average the losses on the days that exceed VaR. VaR answers “how much do I lose at most, usually”; ES answers “how much do I lose on average when it’s bad”. Another common term in reports is maximum drawdown — the fall from the highest point to the lowest — which measures the deepest spill along the path.

4. Hands On: What VaR Looks Like

Suppose your portfolio is worth ¥1 million and its daily return is approximately normal: mean μ = 0.05%, daily volatility σ = 1%. Drag the sliders and watch how the confidence level, volatility, and principal rewrite “how much can I lose”. The red region is the left tail that VaR covers, and its area is exactly 1 − confidence level.

VaR visualized: the left-tail area is exactly 1 − confidence level
VaR quantile: -1.59%VaR amount ≈ ¥15,900About once every 20 trading days
VaR amount = principal × |μ − z·σ|
z is set by the confidence level (90% → 1.2816, 95% → 1.6449, 99% → 2.3263); μ is the average daily return and σ the daily volatility. Check with the default parameters: ¥1,000,000 × |0.05% − 1.6449 × 1%| = ¥1,000,000 × 1.59% ≈ ¥15,900 (¥15,949).

Try two comparisons. Drag the confidence level to 99% (z = 2.3263): the quantile becomes 0.05% − 2.3263% = −2.28%, and VaR ≈ ¥22,800 — the more certainty you demand, the deeper the safety line. Drag σ to 2%: the 95% VaR jumps to about ¥32,400 at once — volatility is VaR’s most sensitive driver; the bumpier the market, the scarier “how much can I lose” gets.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Sort risks into the four types — market, credit, operational, liquidity — and give a real case of each;
  • Read “one-day VaR = ¥1 million at 95% confidence” in full, and convert between confidence levels and “once every N days”;
  • Know that VaR is not the maximum loss and cannot see the tail — ES fills that blind spot;
  • Use VaR ≈ principal × |μ − z·σ| to estimate VaR quickly under the normality assumption.
