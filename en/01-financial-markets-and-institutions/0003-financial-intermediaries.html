The players of the financial system: a field guide to financial intermediaries

About 15 minutes Difficulty: Beginner Prerequisites: Lessons 1–2

The map in Lesson 1 had two boxes: the “financial markets” box was opened up in Lesson 2. This lesson opens the other one — financial intermediaries. The banks, fund companies, insurers, pension funds, and trust and leasing companies around you look like wildly different businesses, yet all of them do the same one thing: they help countless “Auntie Zhangs” cross the three gaps of information, risk, and cost. Learn to recognize all six types of players, and whatever financial need you meet, you will know which door to knock on.

1. Why intermediaries must exist: information asymmetry’s answer

First, a fundamental question: didn’t Lesson 1 say the market’s direct connection is cheaper? Why, then, does most funding in the real world still pass through intermediaries? The answer is information asymmetry. You want to lend out ¥100,000: how do you know the borrower is not a con artist (the ex ante problem)? After the loan is made, might he gamble the money away (the ex post problem)? Lending the whole ¥100,000 to a single person means one default wipes you out (the concentration problem).

Financial intermediaries exist precisely because they aim one tool at each of these three problems:

  • Screening — banks hold credit-bureau data, loan officers, and professional models, and use them to identify who is a reliable borrower before any money goes out;
  • Monitoring — after the loan is made, watching that the money is used as agreed and repaid on time, something an individual lender can hardly do;
  • Risk pooling — gathering small sums from thousands of households into one large pool and lending it out spread across many borrowers, so individual defaults are diluted to near invisibility.
Economies of scale are the common thread

Screening means pulling credit records, monitoring means fielding teams, pooling means running big books — all activities with fixed costs that only spread thin when the money is large enough. One person lending ¥100,000 cannot afford a loan officer; a bank making ten thousand loans can support an entire risk-control department. That is why “intermediaries” can charge a spread, a management fee, a premium: those fees are the rent on economies of scale — and the price you pay for expertise.

2. A field guide to the six types

1. Commercial banks: take deposits, make loans + maturity transformation

Taking deposits from the public and making loans: the oldest and by far the largest type of intermediary. Its most crucial — and most dangerous — job is maturity transformation: deposits can be withdrawn at any moment, while loans, once made, run three to five years. Depositors want “withdrawable anytime”; firms want “long-term and stable.” The bank stands in the middle, absorbing the mismatch and earning the spread for it — at the cost that if depositors all rush to withdraw (a run), the long-term loans on the bank’s books cannot be turned into cash. That is the mechanism of a run crisis, which Branch 02 develops in full.

2. Investment banks: the capital markets’ “construction crew”

They do no deposit-and-loan business; they serve securities issuance and trading: underwriting IPOs and bonds for companies (they starred in Lesson 2’s primary market), brokering mergers, acquisitions, and restructurings, making markets, and providing research and advice. Their income is fees, not the spread — they are paid deal by deal, without tying up their own balance sheets (proprietary trading with their own capital is another matter).

3. Mutual funds and ETFs: pooling small money into a big pool

Thousands of people each put in a few hundred or a few thousand yuan, pooling billions, and a professional fund manager invests the pool in a basket of securities under an agreed strategy. What they solve is the entry threshold of diversification: you have only ¥5,000 and cannot buy a hundred stocks, but one index-fund share buys them all, in proportion. The exchange-traded fund (ETF) pushes the threshold and the fees down to the floor. Funds charge a management fee — accrued on assets under management, win or lose — a feature investors often complain about; Branch 04 will teach you how to read fee schedules.

4. Insurance companies: turning “uncertain huge losses” into “certain small premiums”

No one can predict whether they will have a car accident next year, but across a hundred thousand people next year’s accident rate is remarkably stable — the law of large numbers makes the insurer’s total claims predictable. So it dares to promise: pay a few thousand yuan of premium a year, and if disaster strikes, we pay out hundreds of thousands. An insurer is really two businesses in one: underwriting (collecting premiums, paying claims) plus investing (putting the “float” built up from premiums to work), which also makes it a major institutional investor in the financial markets.

5. Pension funds: the most patient “long money” in the market

Basic public pensions, enterprise annuities, occupational annuities, and personal pension accounts all manage money for a retirement decades away. Their signature advantage is an ultra-long liability horizon: the money goes essentially untouched for decades, so they can absorb short-term volatility in exchange for long-term returns — born contrarian investors, and ballast for the market. When people say “long money” is flowing into the stock market, this is what they mean.

6. Trusts and leasing: customized mainstays on the periphery

Trusts: property managed on trust — a client hands assets to a trust company, which manages and distributes them under the contract (family wealth succession, employee stock ownership, asset segregation) for a trust fee. Leasing: the leasing company buys aircraft, ships, or medical equipment and rents them to firms, which pay rent in installments — “financing through goods instead of money,” easing the strain of one huge purchase.

Intermediaries are not free — and not always well-meaning

Spreads, management fees, and premium loadings are all prices you pay for expertise and scale — reasonable, but in need of oversight. Most financial scams in history wore an intermediary’s coat: unlicensed “wealth-management firms” gathering savings with promises of high interest, Ponzi schemes paying old interest with new money. The way to see through them goes back to Lesson 1 of this branch: draw the funds-flow map and pin down “where exactly does my money go, and who pays my returns?” Branch 08 (Behavioral Finance) and Branch 10 (Personal Finance) will give you a fuller anti-fraud checklist.

✓ Key takeaways
  • You can name the three reasons intermediaries exist — screening (ex ante), monitoring (ex post), and risk pooling (concentration) — all aimed together at information asymmetry;
  • You can capture each institution’s signature craft in one line: banks = maturity transformation, investment banks = underwriting fees, funds = affordable diversification, insurers = the law of large numbers, pensions = patient long money, trusts and leasing = customized stewardship;
  • You can tell apart how each of them earns its money: the spread, underwriting fees, management fees, or premiums and float income;
  • Faced with a concrete financial need, you now ask first “which door should I knock on,” and only then “how much does it charge me, and on what grounds.”
