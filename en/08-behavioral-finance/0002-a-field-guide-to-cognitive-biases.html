Behavioral Finance · Lesson 2

A Field Guide to Cognitive Biases

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1

Lesson 1 showed that the brain habitually handles complex problems with “fast thinking,” and pays for the shortcut with systematic errors. This lesson organizes the six cognitive biases investors meet most often into a “field guide”: each comes with a little story, a finance scenario, and a one-line countermeasure. The goal is not to eliminate biases — they cannot be eliminated — but to make you ask one more question before placing an order: “Am I committing one of these right now?”

1. Opening Experiment: See “Anchoring” With Your Own Eyes

Before meeting the six biases, run a small experiment. It replicates the classic study Kahneman and Tversky published in the journal Science in 1974: a wheel of fortune that spits out a random number, and a general-knowledge question that has nothing to do with the wheel.

A little anchoring experiment: the wheel of fortune and the United Nations
10

The wheel stopped on this number. Remember it, then answer the question below — it has nothing to do with the correct answer.

Whether the wheel lands on 10 or on 65, the number is random and unrelated to the question — yet it visibly dragged both groups’ estimates. That is this lesson’s first bias: anchoring. In investing, your purchase cost is that “wheel-of-fortune number”: it does not change the stock’s value, yet it firmly anchors your decision to “sell only once I break even.”

3. Meet Them One by One: Six Little Stories

1. Anchoring: the market doesn’t know your cost basis

Xiao Lin bought a hot stock at ¥32; it then fell to ¥21. Asked what he planned to do, he said: “The moment it gets back to ¥32 I’ll sell — I’m absolutely not selling before I break even.” The problem: the number 32 means nothing to the market. It is merely Xiao Lin’s own cost — the random “wheel number” spinning in his head. What a stock is worth depends on the company’s future cash flows, not on anyone’s purchase price.

Countermeasure: set buy/sell conditions in advance — tie “sell or not” to cost-unrelated indicators such as fundamental changes or valuation percentiles, write them into your investment journal, and execute when triggered.

2. Availability heuristic: those posting wins are the survivors

The availability heuristic has us estimate probabilities from “the examples that come easily to mind.” Lao Zhang’s coworker posted limit-up screenshots two days in a row; the group chat filled with talk of “financial freedom,” and Lao Zhang thought: so many people really do make money in stocks. What he couldn’t see: those posting their trades are the survivors, while most people who lose stay silent. The saying long circulated in Chinese markets — “seven lose, two break even, one wins”: roughly seven in ten lose, two in ten break even, one in ten profits — describes the whole population of participants.

Countermeasure: fight memorable stories with long-run statistics. In your investment journal, record the gain or loss and the reasoning for every trade; tally the account at year-end and become your own “source of statistics.”

3. Overconfidence: the more you trade, the less you make

The hallmark of overconfidence: after three good calls in a row, you feel you have “a knack for the tape.” That is how Xiao Zhou opened a margin account and chased hot stocks every day. Barber and Odean studied 66,465 individual brokerage accounts (1991–1996) and found that the highest-turnover group earned an average net return about 7 percentage points per year lower than the lowest-turnover group; follow-up research found that men trade more frequently than women and also earn worse net returns (Barber & Odean, 2000/2001). The finding in one sentence: the more frequent the trading, the worse the net return — especially for men.

Countermeasure: make trade frequency rule-based — for example, act only at the quarterly rebalancing; the rest of the time, no watching the tape or placing orders.

4. Confirmation bias: once you own it, all you see is good news

Confirmation bias leads people to gather only supporting evidence once an opinion has formed. After Xiao Wu loaded up on a renewable-energy stock, his follow list was wall-to-wall bullish commentators; he swiped past every bearish report, reassuring himself that “these people don’t understand the industry.” Half a year later the bad news landed, and he discovered the counterevidence had been there all along — he had simply never read it seriously.

Countermeasure: add a mandatory column to your investment journal, “the strongest argument for the other side”; before every intended add-on purchase, first seek out and finish one high-quality bearish article.

5. Mental accounting: the same ¥10,000, different fates

Mental accounting means money carries different labels in the mind. The same ¥10,000: earned as salary, it sits in the account untouched; received as a year-end bonus, it is casually spent on a new phone. It is even more common in investing — a 5% loss on principal keeps you up at night, while a 20% drawdown in “paper gains” goes unnoticed, so the paper profits get carved out to “take a shot.” Yet to your total wealth, a yuan lost from paper gains is exactly a yuan lost from principal.

Countermeasure: consolidate every account into one balance sheet and manage it as a whole; evaluate only the portfolio’s overall risk and return, and give no sum of money “special treatment.”

6. Disposition effect: the self-deception of selling winners and holding losers

Xiao Zheng holds two stocks: one is up 15%, the other down 20%. He sells the winner to “lock in the profit” and keeps the loser to “wait for it to come back” — that is the disposition effect: rushing to realize gains while clinging to losses. It is the direct product of Lesson 1’s loss aversion: as long as you don’t sell, the loss isn’t yet “realized” and psychologically seems not to exist — a self-deception, while the account balance settles honestly every single day.

Countermeasure: set buy/sell conditions in advance, and test every holding with a single question: “If I were all in cash today, would I still buy it at this price?” If the answer is no, sell — regardless of your cost.

Biases ≠ stupidity

These shortcuts are mostly effort-saving and effective in everyday life, and smart people commit them just the same — they are System 1’s default way of working. The goal of learning about biases is not to switch System 1 off, but to deliberately call on System 2 for a check before major decisions. Three all-purpose weapons: rule-based decisions, an investment journal, and pre-set buy/sell conditions.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Name the six common cognitive biases, their symptoms, and the matching finance scenarios
  • Replicate the anchoring experiment yourself and grasp that “even an irrelevant number drags judgment”
  • Remember the empirical evidence: studies of tens of thousands of accounts show that more frequent trading means worse net returns
  • Master the three all-purpose countermeasures: rule-based decisions, an investment journal, pre-set buy/sell conditions
