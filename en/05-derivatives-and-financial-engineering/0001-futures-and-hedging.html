Derivatives & Financial Engineering · Lesson 1

Futures and Hedging

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: 08 Behavioral Finance (bridge)

What an airline fears most is not a high oil price, but an unknown one. The first mission of derivatives is to let a company fix a future price today. In this lesson we follow an airline that must buy 1,000 tons of jet fuel three months from now, and learn forwards, futures, and hedging — and iron the cost curve flat with our own hands.

Derivatives: a contract about the future

derivative is a special kind of contract: it is not an asset itself, but an agreement about the future price of an underlying asset. The underlying can be oil, soybeans, gold, a stock index, an interest rate — even the weather or freight rates.

Two keys unlock derivatives:

  • The value is “derived”: the price of a jet-fuel future tracks the jet-fuel spot price step for step — detached from its underlying, the contract is worthless;
  • The market is zero-sum: every yuan a futures long earns comes out of a short’s pocket. Derivatives trading creates no new value; what it does is transfer risk and redistribute gains and losses.

The derivatives family has three basic building blocks: forwards/futures, options (the star of Lesson 2), and swap (not developed in this course; see the extension signposts on the branch home page). This lesson covers the first.

Forwards and futures: one bloodline, two temperaments

The forward contract is the oldest derivative: buyer and seller privately agree that “three months from now, I will buy 1,000 tons of jet fuel from you at ¥6,000 per ton.” The terms are fully customized, and both parties must perform at maturity.

The futures contract is the “exchange-traded version of a forward”: the contract is standardized — quantity, quality, and delivery date are fixed uniformly — it does not matter who trades with whom, and the exchange stands in the middle as guarantor. The differences between the two come down to four points:

DimensionForward contractFutures contract
Trading venueOver-the-counter (OTC), one-on-one negotiationCentralized exchange auction
Contract termsCustomized: quantity, date, and quality are negotiableStandardized: fixed uniformly by the exchange
SettlementSettled once at maturitymarking to market: gains and losses booked daily
Performance guaranteeRelies on counterparty credit; default risk existsmargin system + clearinghouse guarantee
Early exitDifficult; requires the counterparty’s consentAny time, by closing out with an offsetting trade

Marking to market is the soul of futures: if you buy today at ¥6,000 per ton and the day’s settlement price rises to ¥6,100, a floating gain of ¥100 per ton is credited to your account at once; if it falls, the debit is just as immediate, and a margin shortfall must be topped up. Gains and losses are “settled up” every day, which drives default risk to near zero — at the cost of facing account swings daily.

Hedging: the airline’s 1,000 tons of jet fuel

hedging, also called “offsetting,” comes down to one sentence: take a position in the futures market opposite to your spot-market risk, so that gains and losses on the two sides cancel each other out.

Set the case: an airline must procure 1,000 tons of jet fuel three months from now, and today’s spot price is ¥6,000 per ton. Its nightmare is a fuel-price spike. So today it buys futures at an entry price of K = ¥6,000 per ton, for 1,000 tons. Three months later the contracts expire; consider two scenarios (futures payoff = price at expiration − entry price, per ton):

Item (per ton)Scenario 1: oil rises to 7,000Scenario 2: oil falls to 5,000
Spot-market cost of fuel¥7,000¥5,000
Futures long payoff (entry at 6,000)+¥1,000 (7,000−6,000)−¥1,000 (5,000−6,000)
Combined effective cost7,000 − 1,000 = ¥6,0005,000 + 1,000 = ¥6,000
Total cost for 1,000 tons¥6,000,000¥6,000,000

Step-by-step check: in Scenario 1 the airline pays ¥1,000 more per ton in the spot market and earns ¥1,000 per ton back on the futures (7,000−6,000), so combined 7,000−1,000=¥6,000; in Scenario 2 it saves ¥1,000 per ton in the spot market and loses ¥1,000 per ton on the futures, so combined 5,000+1,000=¥6,000. Both roads lead to the same number — after hedging, the cost always equals the entry price of ¥6,000 per ton, whatever the oil price at expiration.

Post-hedge cost per ton = spot price S − futures gain (S − K) = K
Reading: S is the spot price at expiration; K is the futures entry price. If the oil price rises, the extra paid in the spot market is earned back by the futures; if it falls, the spot-market savings are surrendered to the futures loss. Hedging is not about “getting a bargain” — it is trading away accidental gains to eliminate accidental losses. That is the core function of derivatives: locking tomorrow’s price today.

Speculators: the risk porters take the job

Who takes on the risk the airline wants to offload? The answer is speculators. They hold no jet fuel and need none; they are purely betting on price. In the scenario where oil rises to 7,000, the shorts — mostly speculators — lose ¥1,000 per ton, which is exactly what the hedgers earn. Conversely, when oil falls to 5,000 the speculators profit, pocketing the “accidental gains” the hedgers gave up.

So zero-sum is not a bad thing: hedgers pay money to buy “certainty,” while speculators collect money to sell “certainty” and bet on direction. The risk does not disappear; it merely moves from those who cannot bear it (an airline lives on selling tickets and cannot afford to gamble on fuel prices) to those willing to bear it (speculators betting spare money). Without speculators supplying liquidity, the hedgers’ price-locking orders could never be filled.

⚠ A one-sentence risk warning

Margin carries built-in leverage: a 10% margin requirement means 10× leverage, and a 5% price move swings your capital by 50%. In China’s 2020 “Crude Oil Treasure” incident, investors thought “oil has fallen this low, buying the dip must be safe,” without understanding margin, leverage, or the contract-rollover rules — when the May WTI contract settled at −US$37.63 per barrel, the dip-buyers lost their entire principal and even ended up owing the bank. In 2022, the London “demon nickel” squeeze sent nickel from about US$30,000 per ton to above US$100,000 per ton in two trading days, blowing shorts’ losses through their margin. Until you understand the rules and your maximum loss, stay away from derivatives.

✓ Key takeaways
  • A forward is a customized OTC contract; a futures contract is its standardized + mark-to-market + margin version;
  • Hedging = locking in a future price with an offsetting position: the post-hedge cost always equals the entry price K (¥6,000 per ton in this example);
  • Hedging is zero-sum risk transfer: hedgers buy certainty, while speculators sell certainty and supply liquidity;
  • Leverage cuts both ways: the lessons of the Crude Oil Treasure and demon-nickel episodes — understand the rules and your maximum loss before chasing returns.
