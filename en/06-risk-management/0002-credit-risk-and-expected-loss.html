Risk Management · Lesson 2

Credit Risk and Expected Loss

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 1

Before you lend out ¥1 million, answer three questions: Will the borrower default? If he defaults, how much do I lose? And how much does he owe me in total? This lesson multiplies the three answers together into the expected loss — the number banks compute every day — turning a vague fear of “what if he doesn’t pay” into a multiplication problem you can check.

The Three Building Blocks of Credit Risk: PD, LGD, and EAD

The moment you lend money to a person or a company, or buy a bond, you have taken on credit risk. Taken apart, it consists of three building blocks, and not one of them can be missing.

The first block is probability of default (PD): will he pay back? PD usually uses a one-year observation window, and PD = 2% means: over the coming year, about 2 out of 100 otherwise-similar borrowers will default. It answers “how likely is a default”.

The second block is loss given default (LGD): what share do I lose after a default? Default does not mean losing everything — with real-estate collateral, selling the property recovers a large part and LGD might be only 20%; with unsecured credit loans, collections often recover little, and LGD can run as high as 60%–90%. The pattern is intuitive: the stronger the collateral, the lower the LGD.

The third block is exposure at default (EAD): how much does he owe me in total at the moment of default? EAD is the money actually at risk — the loan principal you have lent out and not yet recovered.

The Core Formula: Expected Loss Is a Multiplication Problem

Multiply the three blocks together and you get expected loss (EL) — how much such a loan loses per loan, on average:

EL = PD × LGD × EAD
Worked example: lend ¥1,000,000 with PD = 2% and LGD = 40%. Check: EL = 2% × 40% × ¥1,000,000 = 0.8% × ¥1,000,000 = ¥8,000. Watch the units: when multiplying the three numbers, PD and LGD must first be written as decimals (0.02 × 0.4 × 1,000,000 = 8,000).

The pricing implication: EL hides inside the interest rate

EL does not vanish into thin air; someone must pay for it. Lending ¥1,000,000 with an EL of ¥8,000 means EL ÷ amount lent = 0.8%: the interest rate must exceed funding and operating costs by at least this 0.8%, or the deal loses money. As an illustrative tally: with funding and operating costs at 2%, the break-even rate floor = 0.8% + 2% = 2.8%; only after adding profit and competitive factors do you reach the final quoted rate.

Why unsecured loan rates run far above mortgage rates

At the same PD, an unsecured credit loan carries a far higher LGD than a mortgage backed by property, so its EL is higher and the risk premium the bank charges is higher too. Interest rates are not set arbitrarily: they must at least cover “average loss + costs + profit”.

Credit Ratings: Letter Labels for Default Probability

A credit rating translates PD into letters anyone can read: AAA means an extremely low likelihood of default, the likelihood climbs notch by notch as you move down the scale, and C/D means already defaulted. In the S&P system, BBB− and above is investment grade, BB+ and below is speculative grade (the familiar “high-yield” or “junk” territory) — a line that is many institutional investors’ threshold for buying at all.

The Portfolio View: Same EL, Wildly Different Volatility

Concentration is credit risk’s amplifier

Plan A: lend the entire ¥1,000,000 to 1 person. Plan B: lend to 100 people, ¥10,000 each (same PD and LGD). The expected loss is exactly the same: under Plan A, EL = 2% × 40% × ¥1,000,000 = ¥8,000; under Plan B, EL per borrower = 2% × 40% × ¥10,000 = ¥80, which totals 80 × 100 = ¥8,000. But the spread of actual outcomes is night and day. Plan A is “all fine or a huge loss” — a 98% chance of walking away whole, a 2% chance of losing ¥400,000 outright (¥1,000,000 × 40%). Plan B’s most likely outcome is about 2 defaults and a loss around ¥8,000 (each default costs ¥10,000 × 40% = ¥4,000), and a total wipeout is nearly impossible. That is the intuition of the binomial distribution (no formula needed): diversification does not reduce expected loss, but it pins outcomes tightly to the expected value — the overnight blow-up is off the table.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Break down any “lending someone money” transaction into its three ingredients: PD, LGD, and EAD
  • Compute EL = PD × LGD × EAD and derive the break-even rate floor = EL ÷ amount lent + costs + profit
  • Read a credit rating table and know that the investment-grade/speculative-grade line sits at S&P’s BBB−
  • Understand concentration: diversification does not change expected loss, but it sharply reduces the volatility of losses
