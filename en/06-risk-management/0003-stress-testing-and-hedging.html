Risk Management · Lesson 3

Stress Testing and Hedging

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 2

VaR tells you “on calm days, the most you lose 95% of the time”. What actually kills portfolios is precisely the other 5%. This lesson learns to imagine the worst case on purpose — stress testing — and to assemble the four response tools: diversification, hedging, insurance, and a capital buffer.

VaR’s Blind Spot: The Invisible Tail

Tail events fall outside the confidence interval

VaR has two weak flanks. First, by its nature it only cares about “inside the interval”, while tail events — the 2008 financial crisis, the March 2020 pandemic circuit-breaker — land precisely outside it: the model says “at 95% confidence you lose at most X”, and inside the remaining 5% hides unlimited loss. Second, it relies on a distribution fitted to historical data, while the fiercest market moves often take shapes history has never shown — a “once-in-a-century” event can arrive twice in a decade. The fall of a portfolio from its peak is called drawdown: in 2008 global stock markets commonly drew down about 50%, and in March 2020 the S&P 500 drew down about 34% in a little over a month.

Stress Testing: Assume the Worst Case Directly

Stress testing sidesteps probabilities and confidence levels and asks one question directly: “If X happens, how much do I lose?” It does not predict whether X will happen; it only makes sure that when X does happen, you are not knocked out on the spot. Scenarios are usually built along two routes, together called scenario analysis:

Historical scenarios: take an extreme period that actually happened and replay it verbatim — for example, the 2008 financial crisis (equities about −50%, government bonds about +5% on safe-haven flows) or the 2020 pandemic crash (equities about −34%, credit bonds sold off too, about −5%). Hypothetical scenarios: ask “what if…?” — for instance, rates +200bp (a 2-percentage-point hike): bond prices fall, monthly payments on outstanding floating-rate mortgages rise, and richly valued growth stocks come under pressure.

A personal stress test

Institutions run stress tests, and so should households. Ask yourself two questions: If I am out of work for 6 months, will the money in my account cover rent or the mortgage plus living expenses? If my income is halved, can the repayments and existing plans still go on? If the answers make you uneasy, what you need is not higher returns but a thicker buffer — an emergency fund of 3–6 months of household expenses is the household’s risk capital.

The last row of the table is the answer to the personal stress test: earmarked emergency savings don’t chase returns — they absorb losses when a shock arrives. They are the household’s capital buffer.

Recovery Math: Avoiding Big Losses Beats Chasing Big Returns

Losses and gains are inherently asymmetric: falling is easy; climbing back is hard. After a fall of x, the gain needed to get back to the starting point is:

Gain needed to recover = decline ÷ (1 − decline)
Check: a 50% decline → 0.5 ÷ 0.5 = 100%; 33.4% → 0.334 ÷ 0.666 ≈ 50.2%; 20% → 0.2 ÷ 0.8 = 25%; 10% → about 11.1%; 90% → 0.9 ÷ 0.1 = 900%. The deeper the decline, the faster the required recovery gain balloons — which is why controlling drawdowns outranks chasing returns.

That is the mathematical case for “prevent the big loss first”: a steady portfolio earning +10% a year beats, over the long run, a boom-bust portfolio that occasionally halves, because −50% needs +100% just to draw even.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Explain VaR’s blind spots: tail events sit outside the confidence interval, and the model depends on a historical distribution
  • Run stress tests with historical and hypothetical scenarios, including the personal version (6 months out of work; income halved)
  • Remember the recovery math: recovery gain = decline ÷ (1 − decline); preventing big losses comes first
  • Assemble the four essentials: diversification, hedging, insurance, and a capital buffer (the emergency fund is the household’s risk capital)
