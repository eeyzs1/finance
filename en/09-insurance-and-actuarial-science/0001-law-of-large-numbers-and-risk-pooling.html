Insurance & Actuarial Science · Lesson 1

Law of Large Numbers and Risk Pooling: Why Insurance Is Possible

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: none

Whether one household suffers a disaster this year, nobody can say; yet ten thousand households added together produce a remarkably stable total of misfortune. Insurance, an ancient business, is built on precisely this mathematical law. This lesson explains it thoroughly and lets you run a risk-pool simulator with your own hands.

One household’s misfortune cannot be predicted

Picture Ms. Wang’s family: this year their old house has a 10% chance of a roughly ¥100,000 accidental loss — fire, a burst pipe, or debris falling from above smashing through the roof. That “10%” is almost useless to the household: by year’s end the loss either happens or it doesn’t; there is no such thing as “a tenth of a fire”. Looking at any single year, Ms. Wang can only sweat it out between dread and hope.

These are exactly the two features of individual risk: whether it strikes is unpredictable, and once it does, the amount is hard to bear. Saving more doesn’t help either — you can’t know which year to save for, or how much would be enough.

Put ten thousand maybes into one pool

Now switch perspectives. Suppose there are 10,000 similar households, independent of one another, each with a 10% chance of one accident this year. Put their fates into a single pool — that is risk pooling.

Ms. Wang alone faces just two outcomes, “0 or ¥100,000”; for the whole pool, the question becomes “how many accidents occur in 10,000 independent trials”. The answer will be tightly concentrated: most likely swinging in a narrow band around 1,000 claims. Once the individual “can’t says” are added up, they turn into the group’s “can say”.

The key premise: independence

The households in the pool must not affect one another — one family’s fire doesn’t spread to the next (in this model). Remember this premise; at the end of the lesson we’ll see what happens when it breaks.

The law of large numbers: a law that compresses volatility

This phenomenon is no coincidence but a rigorously provable theorem — the law of large numbers: the average result of independent repeated events moves ever closer to the expected value as the number of trials grows; and the scale of the fluctuations shrinks at the rate σ/√n.

σ(per person) ≈ σ(one person) / √n
σ(one person) is the standard deviation of a single household’s outcome; n is the number of households in the pool. The expectation hasn’t moved a cent — what changes is the swing around it: quadruple the headcount and √4 = 2, so per-person volatility halves; multiply the headcount by 100 and per-person volatility drops to one-tenth.
Intuition: why exactly √n

Independent households’ deviations point in unrelated directions: some have a bad year, others a lucky one. When you add them up, most of the upward and downward deviations cancel each other, and the residual net volatility scales as exactly √n. The bigger the pool, the cleaner the cancellation.

Work it through: a 10% chance of a ¥10,000 loss per person

To keep the arithmetic easy to do in your head, set every household’s loss at ¥10,000 (swap the story’s ¥100,000 for ¥10,000 and the conclusion is identical — only the number of zeros changes). The number of claims follows a binomial distribution: expected number of claims = np, SD of the count = √(np(1−p)), and each claim pays ¥10,000, so the amounts are just the counts × 10,000. The craft of doing this accounting and pricing for a living is called actuarial science.

Check the SDs: at n = 100, √(100×0.1×0.9) = √9 = 3 claims, i.e. ¥30,000; at n = 10,000, √(10000×0.1×0.9) = √900 = 30 claims, i.e. ¥300,000. Divide by headcount for per-person volatility: ¥300 → ¥30 — exactly one-tenth, because the headcount grew 100-fold while volatility shrank only by √100 = 10.

Notice the most important comparison: no matter how large the pool, the per-person expectation stays ¥1,000 — not a cent saved; what changes is the volatility. Insurance does not sell “a lower expected cost”; it sells “smaller volatility” — and that is exactly where next lesson’s premium pricing begins.

What insurance really is

Insurance does not eliminate risk; it trades an “uncertain large loss” for a “certain small premium”. Nor do insurers profit because they get lucky: for each policyholder, the payout is the violent 0-or-¥10,000; for the whole pool, it is a steady ¥1,000 × headcount. Insurers turn the group’s predictability into a business — collect a certain premium, pay a certain aggregate bill, and stand on the side of the law of large numbers.

Second, the larger n gets, the narrower the histogram and the more it resembles a bell curve — the normal distribution at work underneath. An insurer’s grip on the “95% interval of per-person payouts” comes from exactly here.

The boundary: when risks are no longer independent

The law of large numbers holds only for independent risks

Earthquakes, floods, and pandemics are correlated risks: one event triggers nearly every policy in the pool at once. The “10,000 independent trials” degenerate into “one giant trial”; losses no longer offset one another, and volatility no longer shrinks as √n — a single disaster can punch through the entire pool. The remedy is reinsurance: insurers buy insurance for their own pool, spreading one city’s concentrated earthquake risk across the globe.

This also explains two facts of life: catastrophe cover and pandemic-related lines are expensive and scarce; and only a handful of reinsurance giants worldwide — Munich Re, Swiss Re — are willing to underwrite their tails. Independence is the most expensive premise in the mathematics of insurance.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Understand why individual risk is unpredictable while group risk can be priced (risk pooling)
  • Use σ/√n to gauge volatility: quadruple the headcount and per-person volatility halves
  • Compute expected payouts np, the SD √(np(1−p)), and the 95% interval per person
  • State what insurance really is (a certain small outlay in place of an uncertain large loss) and its limits (correlated risk, reinsurance)
