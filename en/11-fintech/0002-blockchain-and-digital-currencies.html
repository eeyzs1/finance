Blockchain and Digital Currencies

About 18 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1

You have probably heard the word “blockchain” countless times, yet few people explain what problem it actually solves. This lesson is not about trading coins: with nothing more than a classroom ledger game, it makes distributed ledgers, hash pointers, and consensus mechanisms click all at once — and then coolly tallies up the risks.

1. Two Ledgers: The Bank Keeps the Books, or Everyone Does

Start with the scenario you know best. The balance on your bank card is, in essence, one row of numbers in the bank’s database: the bank keeps the books, and you look them up. Why can it be trusted never to mis-record or renege? Because a license, regulators, and the law stand behind it — what you trust is the institution. This is a centralized ledger: one maintainer, trusted by all.

blockchain offers a different answer: have every participant keep an identical copy of the ledger. Each new transaction is broadcast to the whole network; everyone records it and cross-checks. If someone secretly edits their copy the next day, everyone else’s ledgers expose them at once. The beauty is that participants need not trust each other at all to reconcile a single consistent ledger — even mutual distrust can still reconcile the books.

A one-line mantra

Decentralization does not mean “nobody is in charge”; it means “no single party can unilaterally alter the books”.

DimensionBank ledger (centralized)Blockchain ledger (distributed)
Who keeps the booksOnly the bank; customers can only view their own accountsEvery node on the network keeps a copy; anyone can reconcile
Where trust comes fromTrust in the institution, regulation, and lawTrust in rules and cryptography; no mutual trust needed
Single point of failureIf the central system goes down, service is disruptedAny node can go offline; the others still hold the full ledger
Difficulty of altering the booksAn insider with access could, in theory, change themChanging one block means redoing all later blocks and overpowering most nodes

2. Blocks and Hash Pointers: Why Changing One Block Means Redoing Them All

A blockchain packs a window of transactions into a “block”, like one page of a ledger. Each page records three things: the batch of transactions, the timestamp at packing time, and the hash of the previous block — that last thread is called the hash pointer.

A hash function works like a juicer: whether you drop in a whole novel or a single number, what comes out is a fixed-length “fingerprint”; change so much as one punctuation mark in the original and the fingerprint becomes unrecognizable; and the original cannot be recovered from the fingerprint.

Block hash = H (this block’s transactions ‖ timestamp ‖ previous block’s hash)
H is the hash function; ‖ means the contents are concatenated. Real blockchains (such as Bitcoin) use SHA-256, whose output is 64 hexadecimal characters; the demonstrator below switches to a simplified 8-character hash so you can eyeball it.

So the blocks interlock, one ring inside the next: block 2 records block 1’s fingerprint, block 3 records block 2’s… Sneak a single character into block 1 and block 1’s hash changes on the spot; yet the “previous block hash” recorded in block 2 is still the old value and no longer matches — from block 2 onward the whole chain unravels. What “tamper-proof” really means

The precise statement is “tampering is prohibitively expensive”, not absolutely impossible: an attacker must redo every block after the altered one and control a majority of the network’s bookkeeping nodes (for example, overpowering 51% of its hash power) before the network might accept the rewritten chain. The smaller the network, the more fragile; the larger, the closer to truly tamper-proof — smaller networks really have suffered majority-hash-power attacks.

3. Consensus Mechanisms: Whose Ledger Governs

A new problem arrives: everyone keeps the books, so whose copy counts? The rules that decide this are called the consensus mechanism. The two most common:

Proof of work (PoW): the whole network pits computing power against a puzzle with no shortcuts; whoever solves it first packs the next block and claims the reward — bookkeeping rights are bought with real money spent on electricity. Bitcoin uses this.

Proof of stake (PoS): bookkeeping rights are drawn by lot, weighted by how many coins you stake and for how long; misbehavior gets your stake slashed — energy use is dramatically lower, at the cost of giving bigger holders more say. Ethereum moved from PoW to PoS in September 2022.

Remember in one sentence

PoW buys trust with electricity; PoS posts its coins as collateral.

4. The Crypto-Asset Spectrum: Don’t Confuse Three Different Things

“Assets that run on a blockchain” are often lumped together as “coins”, but they actually sit along a spectrum: at one end, Bitcoin with its fully decentralized issuance; at the other, a central bank digital currency backed by national credit; in between, the stablecoin anchored to fiat money.

5. A Risk Checklist: Blockchain ≠ Guaranteed Profit

The mechanics are done — now for three buckets of cold water. The real risks ordinary people face when touching “on-chain assets” include at least these:

Three buckets of cold water

① Violent price swings: a 10% rise or fall in a single day is not rare for Bitcoin — never participate with money you will need next month; ② Regulatory uncertainty: policies differ greatly across jurisdictions and keep changing, and an asset’s compliance status can shift at any time; ③ Platforms and scams: exchange hacks, platforms that vanish, fake coins, and “guaranteed high-return” schemes are endless — on-chain transfers are irreversible, and a lost private key cannot be reissued. In one sentence: blockchain ≠ guaranteed profit.

Technology is neutral

Distributed ledgers themselves are being put to serious uses such as trade settlement, supply-chain traceability, and bond registration. Look at “ledger technology” and “coin speculation” separately, and you will stay much cooler-headed.

✓ Key takeaways
  • See the essential difference between the two ledgers: the centralized one rests on trusting an institution; the distributed one rests on rules and cryptography, reconciling books even amid mutual distrust
  • Explain how blocks interlock through the “previous block hash”, and why changing one block means redoing them all
  • State the PoW vs. PoS difference in one sentence: one buys trust with electricity, the other posts its coins as collateral
  • Place Bitcoin, stablecoins, and central bank digital currencies on a single spectrum instead of lumping them together
  • Remember the three risks: violent volatility, shifting regulation, platforms and scams — blockchain ≠ guaranteed profit
