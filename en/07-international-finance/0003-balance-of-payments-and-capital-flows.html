International Finance · Lesson 3

Balance of Payments and Capital Flows

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: Lesson 2

Why do exchange rates move? Last lesson's answer was “money is flowing”. This lesson opens the ledger of those flows — the balance of payments: it shows where every payment between a country and the world comes from and where it goes. Read this ledger well and you hold the force behind exchange rates — and you can follow the drama of a “currency crisis” as it plays out.

1. A Ledger That Always Balances

The balance of payments account (BOP) records all of a country's residents' transactions with the rest of the world over a period: a shipment of exported phones, one share of a US stock bought, a foreign firm setting up a plant in China, the central bank buying US Treasuries… every entry goes in the book. It uses double-entry bookkeeping: each transaction is entered once on the debit side and once on the credit side, so the whole table must sum to zero — the ledger always balances; the only question is “balanced by what”.

The ledger falls into three blocks: the current account, which records trade in goods and services; the capital and financial account, which records cross-border purchases and sales of assets; and the change in the central bank's pocket — the change in reserve assets.

2. One Identity, Two Destinies

Current account balance + capital and financial account balance + change in reserve assets = 0
The three blocks must sum to zero. A surplus country necessarily “lends to the world” in some form: either private-sector investment abroad, or an accumulation of central bank reserves — the ledger allows no third way.

Application one: China's export-surplus era. Exports grew at high speed through the 2000s, and in 2007 — the peak year — the current account surplus approached 10% of GDP. Where did such a huge surplus go? Part became outward investment by firms and households (a capital and financial account deficit); the larger part was absorbed by the central bank buying foreign exchange — foreign exchange reserves climbed from about US$0.17 trillion in 2000 to a peak of nearly US$4 trillion in 2014.

Application two: America's “thirty-year deficit”. The US current account has not posted a single surplus year since 1991; in 2023 the deficit was about US$820 billion, roughly 3% of GDP. What fills the gap? The rest of the world persistently buying dollar assets net — a capital and financial account surplus. This is the so-called “exorbitant privilege”: because the world accepts its currency, it can consume on credit for decades. But the privilege is also a hidden danger: once confidence wavers and funds flee, the dollar and US assets come under pressure together.

3. Crisis Sketch: Thailand, 1997

Currency crisis sketch: the Thai baht, 1997

Three things stacked that year: a fixed exchange rate + a chronic current account deficit + reliance on short-term foreign debt. The current account deficit was propped up by short-term dollar borrowing (a capital and financial account surplus filled the gap); once exports weakened and markets doubted the fixed rate could hold, capital fled in panic; the central bank defended the rate with its reserves, but they were far too thin against the short-term debt and were exhausted within weeks. On July 2, 1997 Thailand was forced to abandon the fixed rate, and the baht fell from about 25 to the dollar to around 50 by early the next year — nearly halved. The depreciation then inflated the local-currency value of foreign debt, firms and banks fell in a chain, and the crisis spread across Asia. The lesson: filling a current account hole with “hot” money is the most fragile kind of balance.

4. The Savings View: S − I = CA

A national accounts identity: the current account balance = national saving − domestic investment. A surplus country has not simply “won” — it saves more than it invests at home and lends the difference to the world; a deficit country invests more than it saves and borrows from the world to fill the gap. Next time you see the word “surplus”, what should come to mind is “net saving abroad”, not a simple victory.

✓ Key takeaways
  • The BOP is the ledger of all a country's transactions with the world, kept by double entry, always summing to zero.
  • Three blocks: the current account (goods + services + primary income + transfers), the capital and financial account (FDI + portfolio + other), and the change in reserve assets.
  • The identity CA + capital and financial account + reserve change = 0 — China's surplus era ended in reserve accumulation; America's thirty-year deficit is sustained by the world holding dollar assets.
  • Thailand 1997: fixed rate + current deficit + short-term debt → capital flight → reserves exhausted → devaluation.
  • S − I = CA: a surplus = net saving abroad, the difference lent to the world.
