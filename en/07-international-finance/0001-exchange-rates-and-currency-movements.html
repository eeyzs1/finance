Exchange Rates and Currency Movements: What Does 7.2 Actually Mean?

About 15 minutesDifficulty: AdvancedPrerequisites: none

The news says “the renminbi exchange rate has reached 7.2”, and some rejoice while others despair: parents of students abroad breathe easier, while the toy-factory boss frowns. In 15 minutes this lesson makes three things clear: what an exchange rate actually is, how to tell appreciation from depreciation, and why the very same appreciation is diametrically opposite news for different people.

1. The Exchange Rate: A Currency Priced in Another Currency

An apple's price is quoted in renminbi; the renminbi's price is quoted in US dollars. The exchange rate is a currency's conversion ratio: USD/CNY = 7.2 reads as “1 US dollar exchanges for ¥7.2”. USD is the international code for the US dollar, and CNY is the international code for the renminbi.

Writing the price with the foreign currency fixed at 1, expressed as a number of home-currency units, is called direct quotation. The trick for assigning roles: the currency before the slash is the “good”, and the currency after the slash is the “ruler” — just as in “apples at ¥3 per 500 g”, the apple is the good being priced and the yuan is the ruler that measures it.

How to read it

USD/CNY = 7.2: the dollar is the good, the renminbi is the ruler, and 7.2 is the renminbi price of the good called “one US dollar”. Likewise EUR/CNY = 7.8 means one euro sells for ¥7.8.

2. Appreciation or Depreciation: Ask Just One Question

Exchange rates move every day, but there is nothing to memorize — just ask one question: is one US dollar buying more renminbi, or less? Less means the renminbi is worth more — an appreciation; more is a depreciation.

The direction test (the core of this lesson)

USD/CNY moves from 7.2 to 6.8: one US dollar buys ¥6.8 instead of ¥7.2 — less — so the renminbi has appreciated (and the dollar has depreciated against it). Conversely, a move from 7.2 to 8.0 means one US dollar buys more renminbi — the renminbi has depreciated.

The most common misreading

“It rose from 7.2 to 7.4 — the renminbi appreciated!” Wrong. Under direct quotation (foreign currency fixed at 1), a bigger number is precisely home-currency depreciation. First see clearly which currency is the good and which is the ruler; only then talk about direction.

3. Worked Through: An Exporting Toy Factory's Make-or-Break Line

A coastal factory exports plush toys: each piece costs ¥6.3 to make and targets ¥0.5 of profit, so it must take in ¥6.8 per piece; the US distributor can accept a purchase price of about US$0.95 per piece. The dollar price quoted to the US customer = renminbi to be recovered ÷ exchange rate:

Break-even US$ quote = (cost per piece + target profit) ÷ USD/CNY = 6.8 ÷ 7.2 ≈ US$0.944 per piece
Default check: at an exchange rate of 7.2, the quote is about US$0.944 — below the market-acceptable US$0.95 — so the order holds. If the renminbi appreciates to 6.8, the quote becomes 6.8 ÷ 6.8 = US$1.000 and the order flies away at once. The make-or-break line sits at 6.8 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 7.16: once the rate falls below it, the factory can no longer quote a competitive price.

One and the same event hits different parties in completely opposite ways. Before judging the impact, ask yourself: am I the party receiving foreign currency, or the party paying it? Receivers of foreign currency (exporters, overseas workers) fear home-currency appreciation; payers of foreign currency (importers, families of students abroad, holders of foreign-currency debt) welcome it.

5. Beyond the Nominal Rate: One Honest Sentence and One Mechanism

The nominal exchange rate is the number on the bank's screen; the real exchange rate adds an adjustment for the two countries' prices. If the renminbi appreciates 5% against the dollar while domestic toy factory-gate prices also rise 5%, the real price facing the American buyer has barely moved — a nominal appreciation does not necessarily mean a real loss of competitiveness. When judging effects on trade, the real exchange rate is the more accurate ruler.

One-line extension: the RMB central parity mechanism

Before the market opens each trading day, the China Foreign Exchange Trade System publishes the central parity rate of the renminbi against the US dollar, and the onshore spot rate for that day may fluctuate within a band around the parity — this is the “central parity mechanism” so often mentioned in the news: the renminbi exchange rate is neither fully fixed nor freely floating.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Read USD/CNY = 7.2 correctly: the foreign currency is the good, the home currency is the ruler
  • Judge direction in one sentence: one US dollar buying fewer renminbi = renminbi appreciation
  • Compute an exporter's break-even quote: 6.8 ÷ 7.2 ≈ US$0.944, with the make-or-break line at 6.8 ÷ 0.95 ≈ 7.16
  • Remember that the impact of appreciation and depreciation always splits by party: foreign-currency receivers fear appreciation, foreign-currency payers welcome it
