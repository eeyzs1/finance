Financial Markets & Institutions · Lesson 1

The journey of funds: direct and indirect finance

About 15 minutes Difficulty: Beginner Prerequisites: none (start of this branch)

Every day, countless funds move between strangers: Auntie Zhang’s savings become the second production line at Boss Li’s bakery, and behind your mortgage stand the savings of thousands of households. The first problem the financial system has to solve is moving funds from people with spare money to projects that need money. There are two main routes for the journey: transfer through a bank, or straight across a market. In this lesson we follow ¥100 of savings down each road in turn.

1. Who needs money, and who has it

Let’s first sketch the roles at the two ends.

Surplus units are usually households: once wages arrive and spending is done, money is left over; kept in savings, they fear it will lose value, and investing it themselves is beyond them. Taken as a whole, the household sector is the economy’s largest net saver.

Deficit units are mainly firms and governments: firms want to expand output, do R&D, and build plants — the returns look good but cash on hand falls short; governments must build roads and schools, spending ahead of tax receipts. They need “today’s money” to earn “tomorrow’s returns.”

A plain but stubborn problem

Auntie Zhang has ¥100,000 of spare money; Boss Li’s bakery wants to borrow ¥500,000 to open a second shop. Auntie Zhang doesn’t know Boss Li, and she has no way to judge a bakery’s business; Boss Li, for his part, cannot go knocking on doors to borrow it. Between the two of them lie three gaps: information (who can tell who is trustworthy), risk (what if the money never comes back), and cost (matching small sums one by one is far too expensive). The solutions for crossing these three gaps make up the entire financial system.

Two main roads cross the gaps: hand your money to an intermediary institution that lends it out for you (indirect finance), or buy outright a security that a firm issues (direct finance).

2. The two roads: indirect finance and direct finance

Route A: indirect finance — Auntie Zhang deposits her money at a bank, and the bank lends it to Boss Li. Note the defining feature: Auntie Zhang deals only with the bank (she receives deposit interest), and Boss Li owes only the bank (he pays loan interest); the bank stands in the middle and bears the credit risk of default itself. What the bank earns is the difference between the two rates.

Route B: direct finance — Boss Li skips the bank and issues securities directly on the market: issuing stock invites Auntie Zhang in as a part-owner, or issuing bonds borrows from her outright. Borrowers sell securities directly to surplus units through the financial market, with no bank transferring the money.

Take the market road and the bank no longer stands in the middle collecting the difference — but the market itself is not free either: issuing securities costs underwriting fees, and trading costs commissions (Lesson 3 introduces the institutions that live on those fees). For now, this lesson simplifies the market channel to zero cost so the core contrast stands out.

Bank’s annual spread income = loans outstanding × (lending rate − deposit rate)
With a 2% deposit rate and a 5% lending rate, the spread is 3 percentage points: on ¥60 of loans, the bank earns 60 × 3% = ¥1.8 a year. That ¥1.8 is return given up by savers and extra cost paid by firms — it is the price of the “transfer service.”

Third, on the gold channel the 4.5% savers receive is exactly equal to the 4.5% firms pay — the market squeezes out the middleman’s markup, and the price is that savers must face the risk of the firm not repaying on their own.

3. What each method is good and bad at

If the market’s direct connection is “cheaper,” why haven’t banks been competed out of existence? Because each road wins on its own ground. The heart of the matter is information asymmetry:

DimensionIndirect finance (banks)Direct finance (markets)
Information processing Banks screen borrowers professionally and keep monitoring after the loan; Auntie Zhang need not understand the bakery business Investors must judge securities themselves (or buy ratings); the information cost lands on each of them individually
Risk bearing The bank absorbs default risk; depositors are barely touched Investors bear default and price swings directly, and can lose every cent
Cost of funds The spread is permanent — a “toll” shared by savers and borrowers alike No spread to pay; for large, high-quality issuers, bond funding usually costs less than bank loans
Thresholds and flexibility Small deposits go in and out at will; the bank can even handle small loans one by one Issuing securities has scale and disclosure thresholds that keep small firms out
Demands on the firm No need to publish operating details; terms are negotiated one-on-one with the bank Must disclose financial statements and accept continuous market pricing (which is also a discipline)
Remember this division of labor

Banks are good at handling money that is small, scattered, and information-hard — exactly what households’ scattered savings needs; markets are good at financing that is large, standardized, and information-transparent — which is why those issuing stocks and bonds are mostly mature, large firms. In reality the two roads coexist, and their relative size varies with how a financial system is structured: systems where markets carry more (as in the United States) are called market-based financial systems, and those where banks carry more (as in China, Germany, and Japan) are called bank-based financial systems.

4. The financial system map at a glance

Put the two roads on one map, add all the supporting players, and you have the skeleton of the entire financial system. Surplus units stand on the left, deficit units on the right, and between them run the two channels: financial intermediaries and financial markets. Above them sit the central bank and the regulators, who make the rules and act as lender of last resort; the return line along the bottom reminds you — funds are not a one-way ticket, and interest, dividends, and claims flow back to surplus units in a steady stream.

This map rewards a few extra looks: Lesson 2 of this branch zooms into the “financial markets” box (primary/secondary markets), Lesson 3 zooms into the “financial intermediaries” box (six types of institutions), and the next branch zooms into the “central bank.”

✓ Key takeaways
  • You can point out the surplus units (households) and the deficit units (firms, government), and name the three gaps between them: information, risk, and cost;
  • You can tell indirect finance from direct finance at a glance by asking whether a bank stands in the middle bearing the credit risk;
  • You can compute the spread with the formula: ¥60 × (5% − 2%) = ¥1.8, and explain it as return savers give up and extra cost firms pay;
  • You now hold the financial system map in your head: surplus units → (intermediaries | markets) → deficit units, with regulation overhead and returns flowing back below.
