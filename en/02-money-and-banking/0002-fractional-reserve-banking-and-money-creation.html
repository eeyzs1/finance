Money & Banking · Lesson 2

Fractional Reserve Banking and Money Creation

About 20 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1 · The Nature and Measurement of Money

Deposit ¥1,000 at a bank and it will not lock the money in a vault. Through round after round of “lend — return — lend again,” the banking system as a whole can, at most, turn that ¥1,000 into ¥5,000 of deposit money. This lesson takes the mechanism apart step by step — a mechanism that is easily misunderstood yet critically important: money creation under fractional reserve banking.

1. A Bank Is Not a Vault

Many people imagine it like this: the bank takes your deposit, puts it in the vault, and waits for you to withdraw it — the bank is merely a “custodian of funds.” The reality: a modern commercial bank is closer to a “machine for circulating and creating funds.” When it receives a deposit, it keeps only a small slice as reserves, according to the required reserve ratio, and lends the rest out; once the borrower spends the money, it does not vanish — it flows back into another bank (or the same one) as a new deposit; the new deposit is partly kept as reserves and partly lent again… and so the cycle repeats.

The core mechanism

Under fractional reserve banking, “loans create deposits”: a loan extended becomes a new deposit, and the new deposit supports yet another loan. The banking system's total deposits end up far larger than the cash that first flowed in.

2. Round by Round: How ¥100 Rolls into ¥500

Assume an initial deposit of ¥100 and a reserve ratio of 20%. Round 1: the bank takes the ¥100, keeps ¥20 as reserves, and lends ¥80. Round 2: the ¥80 flows back into a bank as a new deposit; ¥16 is kept, ¥64 is lent. Round 3: the ¥64 returns; ¥12.8 is kept, ¥51.2 is lent… A deposit formed by loaned money flowing back (as opposed to the original cash deposit) is called a derivative deposit.

3. The Money Multiplier: The Theoretical Ceiling

The multiple by which each ¥1 of initial deposit is ultimately “amplified” is called the money multiplier. The cash and bank reserves at the starting point of the cycle together are the monetary base — the “original ammunition” of the whole derivation process.

Money multiplier m = 1 ÷ reserve ratio rr　　Deposit ceiling = initial deposit × m
Note: this is a theoretical ceiling. It assumes every loan flows back into the banking system, banks hold no excess reserves, and each round has enough loan demand — in reality the three rarely hold at once (see the next section).

Plug in the numbers: at a 20% reserve ratio, m = 1÷0.2 = 5, so ¥100 → ¥500; at 10%, m = 10, so ¥100 → ¥1,000; raise the ratio to 50% and m is only 2. The higher the reserve ratio, the more money “leaks” out of the cycle and the smaller the multiple — one reason this is a lever the central bank uses to steer the money supply (more in the next lesson).

4. Reality Check: Why the Actual Multiplier Is Much Smaller

Three “leakages”

① Cash drain: people hold part of the money as cash, so it never returns to a bank; ② excess reserves: for safety, banks hold reserves above the legal minimum; ③ weak loan demand: in a slump, banks want to lend but nobody wants to borrow, and the cycle stalls midway. All three cut real-world money creation down to size.

Combine the cash-drain rate and the excess-reserve rate into c, and the actual multiplier is roughly 1÷(rr+c). Example: with a 10% required reserve ratio, if drain plus excess reserves total 15%, the actual multiplier ≈ 1÷(0.10+0.15) = 4, far below the theoretical 10. That is why, when looking at the real money supply (M2), you cannot simply apply 1/rr.

5. A Key Clarification: Banks Cannot “Print Money for Their Own Spending”

The most common misconception

“Banks create money” ≠ “banks print money and spend it on themselves.” For every ¥1 a bank lends, it records a ¥1 deposit on the liability side — assets and liabilities rise together, and the bank's own net worth has not gained a single penny out of thin air. What banks create is “deposit money” (ledger money you can pay by transfer); their profits can come only from the spread between deposit and loan rates, and they remain bound by reserves, liquidity, and regulation. The only institution that can truly create monetary base “out of thin air” is the central bank — the protagonist of the next lesson.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Understand the multi-round “loans create deposits” cycle
  • Derive the deposit ceiling with a geometric series (¥100 → ¥500)
  • Remember that the money multiplier = 1 ÷ the reserve ratio, and that it is only a theoretical ceiling
  • Name the three reasons the actual multiplier falls short: cash drain, excess reserves, weak loan demand
  • Clear up the misconception that “banks print money to spend on themselves”
