Money & Banking · Lesson 1

The Nature and Measurement of Money

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: none

What is money? Your first thought is probably the balance on your phone or the notes in your pocket. This lesson starts from money's three functions, first revisiting a barter world with no money to see why money was bound to appear; it then follows cowries, coins, and paper notes all the way to today's digital payments; and finally clarifies what the central bank is actually counting when it talks about M0, M1, and M2. By the end, you will be able to answer: what exactly is the difference between the money in WeChat Pay and cash?

1. What Is Money: The Three Functions

Finance's definition of money is surprisingly plain: anything generally accepted in payment counts as money — whether cowries, gold, or a string of digits on your phone. To judge whether something qualifies as “money,” see whether it performs all three of the functions below.

Function 1: Medium of exchange — the moment you scan to pay

The medium of exchange is the “middle thing” that both sides of a trade will accept. You scan a code at the convenience store to pay ¥15 for breakfast: the shopkeeper is not taking your old sofa — they are taking money that everyone recognizes. With a medium of exchange, the fish seller no longer has to stumble upon a “baker who happens to want fish”; the barrier to trade drops to the floor.

Function 2: Unit of account — the price on the menu

The unit of account is the shared yardstick that measures all prices. The menu says “beef noodles ¥22,” and you instantly know how much pricier that is than the ¥18 vegetable noodles — the value of every good is converted into one unit, “yuan,” just as a ruler standardizes length. Without this yardstick, working out how many fish, how many pairs of shoes, or how many bags of salt a phone is worth would be an endless chain of conversions.

Function 3: Store of value — the money in Yu'e Bao

The store of value lets purchasing power travel through time. Your salary is not spent the day it arrives; the money in Yu'e Bao can still be spent next month or next year — money stores “today's labor” as “tomorrow's purchasing power.” Of course, inflation quietly erodes that store, a story later lessons will unpack.

The one-sentence test

Can pay the bill (medium of exchange), can price the goods (unit of account), can be kept for later spending (store of value) — all three together make something money in the full sense. A credit card limit can pay the bill, but it is not your money: it is borrowed money.

2. A World Without Money: The Barter Problem

Imagine the day money disappears: a fisherman catches fish and wants bread for dinner; the baker has baked bread but walking has worn out his shoes and he wants a new pair; the shoemaker has finished the shoes and wants fish to eat after work. The three men's supplies and wants form a perfect-looking circle — or do they?

The fisherman first takes fish to the baker, who shakes his head: “I don't eat fish.” The baker takes bread to the shoemaker, who waves him off: “I have plenty of bread at home.” The shoemaker takes shoes to the fisherman, who shrugs: “I don't need shoes.” Three attempts, three failures. Economists call this hurdle the double coincidence of wants: for a trade to happen, “you happen to have what I want” and “I happen to have what you want” must hold at the same time. The condition is so strict it almost never occurs — so most exchanges simply never take place.

Money was born precisely to break this deadlock: as long as everyone believes “money” can be exchanged for goods, trade no longer needs coincidence — just a counterparty willing to accept money, and such people are everywhere.

3. The Evolution of Money: From Cowries to the Digital Yuan

Money was not “invented” one day; it was selected, over and over, across thousands of years. The table below shows its five main forms:

FormExamplesKey featuresWhy it is accepted
Commodity moneyCowries, salt, clothHas use value in its own right; being money is a side jobScarce, durable, universally wanted
Metal coinsCopper cash, silver ingots, gold coinsStable in value, divisible, easy to carryThe metal itself is valuable; purity and weight can be verified
Paper money (gold standard)Banknotes redeemable in goldLight and portable, but issuance was constrained by gold reservesA promise to redeem in gold at a fixed rate on demand
Fiat moneyRenminbi, US dollar, Japanese yenAlmost no intrinsic value of its ownState credit stands behind it + legal-tender laws force acceptance
Electronic payments and central bank digital currencyWeChat Pay, the digital yuan (e-CNY)A number on a ledger; payment settles instantlyEquivalent to cash (see the FinTech branch)

Notice the turning point: the ¥100 note in your wallet costs far less to print than its face value and cannot be exchanged for gold. It still buys ¥100 of goods because the state's credit backs it, the law requires it to be accepted, and the central bank controls its quantity. Money has gone from “a commodity valuable in itself” to “a trusted bookkeeping symbol” — digital payments simply push that logic to its extreme: money becomes numbers flickering on a screen.

4. How Much Money Is There: The Aggregates M0 / M1 / M2

Money comes in many forms, so to “count it” the central bank layers it by how easily it converts to cash. The key concept here is liquidity: cash is already cash, so it is the most liquid; a time deposit must wait until maturity or be withdrawn early; a house sells slowly and possibly at a discount, so it is less liquid.

The three layers nest outward: M0 is cash in circulation (notes and coins held by the public, excluding vault cash inside the banking system); M1 (narrow money) = M0 + demand deposits — money you can spend right away, representing active purchasing power; M2 (broad money) wraps on another layer of “quasi-money” — time deposits, savings deposits, and the like — which must be withdrawn before it can be spent, representing potential purchasing power.

M2 = M1 + time deposits + savings deposits + other deposits (quasi-money)
Definitions differ slightly across countries: for example, from its January 2025 data onward, the People's Bank of China includes personal demand deposits and non-bank payment institutions' customer reserve funds in M1; the United States also folded savings deposits into M1 in 2020. Always defer to the definitions published by your own central bank.

That raises the boundary question of “what counts as money”: which layer does WeChat change (lingqian) belong to? It is backed by reserve funds deposited by the payment institution and can be spent with a scan, so it behaves like a demand deposit — under China's new definitions from 2025 it is already counted in M1; whereas money-market fund products such as Yu'e Bao involve a settlement lag on redemption, making them “edge money” outside M2, though most of their underlying assets flow back into bank deposits. In the digital age, the boundary of money is getting blurrier and blurrier.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Use the three functions (medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value) to judge what is money and what is not.
  • Explain why the double coincidence of wants makes barter grind to a halt, and how money breaks the deadlock.
  • Retell money's evolution from cowries and coins to paper, fiat money, and digital payment, and understand how state credit replaced gold.
  • Distinguish the nesting of M0 / M1 / M2, and say which layer a time deposit and WeChat change each belong to.
