Compounding and Inflation: Two Faces of Time

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: none

Time is the most powerful variable in personal finance: it can make money breed money, and it can quietly make money thinner. This lesson works through three sets of numbers that put compound interest and inflation side by side; by the end, “start early” will be burned into your muscle memory.

1. Compound Interest: Interest That Earns Interest

Under simple interest, interest is forever computed on the original principal alone. Compound interest folds each period’s interest into the principal, so the next period charges interest on principal plus interest together — colloquially, “interest on interest.” Its growth formula is a single line:

FV = PV × (1 + r)^n
FV = future value; PV = principal (present value); r = rate of return per period (as a decimal — 7% is written 0.07); n = number of periods.

Plug in one case: ¥10,000 at 7% a year — after 10 years, 1 × 1.07^10 ≈ 1.97, about ¥19,700; after 30 years, 1 × 1.07^30 ≈ 7.61, about ¥76,100. The principal never changed and the return never changed; simply stretching time from 10 years to 30 nearly quadruples the ending value: the great majority of compounding’s payoff piles up in the final few years.

Verify it yourself

1.07^2 = 1.1449 → 1.07^4 = 1.1449 squared ≈ 1.3108 → 1.07^5 ≈ 1.4026 → 1.07^10 = 1.4026 squared ≈ 1.9672 → 1.07^20 ≈ 1.9672 squared ≈ 3.8697 → 1.07^30 = 3.8697 × 1.9672 ≈ 7.6123. A phone calculator can reproduce every step.

Doubling, computed in your head

rule of 72: years to double ≈ 72 ÷ annual return (%). At 7% → about 10.3 years to double (the exact answer, ln2 ÷ ln1.07 ≈ 10.24 years — off by less than 1%); at 3% → about 24 years; at 12% → about 6 years. The higher the return, the faster the doubling — that is why you should care about the rate of return.

2. Simple vs. Compound: ¥180,000 Apart over 30 Years

How big is the gap? Take ¥100,000, an annual interest rate of 5%, and a 30-year deposit, and set the two head to head:

Interest methodFormulaValue after 30 years
Simple interest¥100,000 × (1 + 5% × 30)¥250,000
Compound interest¥100,000 × 1.05^30¥432,000

Check: 1.05^10 = 1.6289 → 1.05^20 ≈ 2.6533 → 1.05^30 ≈ 4.3219, so the compound value = ¥100,000 × 4.322 = ¥432,000; simple interest = ¥100,000 × 5% × 30 = ¥150,000, for an ending value of ¥250,000. The extra ¥182,000 or so from compounding is almost entirely “interest on interest” — and the longer the term, the larger its share.

3. Inflation: The Other Face of Time

Beyond the engine there is a headwind. Inflation slowly shrinks what each yuan can buy, so a return must be measured net of inflation: what you truly earn after stripping out price changes is the real return.

Real return ≈ nominal return − inflation
The Fisher approximation; the exact form is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1 — at everyday interest rates the two are nearly identical.
Don’t be soothed by nominal numbers

A deposit paying a nominal 3% earns a real return of just 0.5% at 2.5% inflation; cash stuffed under the mattress loses about −2.4% a year in real terms, so after 30 years ¥10,000 keeps only about ¥4,767 of purchasing power — nearly halved. “Safety” that cannot outrun inflation is, in fact, a slow-motion loss.

4. The Power of Starting Early: Time Is Worth More Than Amount

Dollar-cost averaging is the lowest-effort way for ordinary people to put compounding to work: invest a fixed amount every payday, and let discipline replace market timing. Using the simplified convention of “¥1,000 per month, ¥12,000 invested at each year-end, compounded annually at 7%,” compare three starting ages (all investing through age 60):

FV = C × ((1 + r)^n − 1) ÷ r
Future value of an ordinary annuity: C = contribution per year, r = annual return, n = number of years. Simplified here to a single year-end contribution with annual compounding, so it is easy to check by hand.
Starting ageYears investedTotal contributedValue at 60 (approx.)
Age 2535 years¥420,000¥1,659,000
Age 3525 years¥300,000¥759,000
Age 4515 years¥180,000¥302,000

Check: 1.07^35 ≈ 10.677, 1.07^25 ≈ 5.427, 1.07^15 ≈ 2.759. Plugging into the annuity formula: ¥12,000 × 138.24 ≈ ¥1,659,000; ¥12,000 × 63.25 ≈ ¥759,000; ¥12,000 × 25.13 ≈ ¥302,000. With exact monthly compounding all three figures would be higher (about ¥1.8 million over 35 years); the table keeps the annual simplification so you can verify it by hand.

The person who starts at 25 contributes only ¥240,000 more than the one who starts at 45, yet ends up with about ¥1,360,000 more — the gap comes mostly from time, not from the amount. Starting early simply gives compounding a longer runway.

✓ Key takeaways
  • You can estimate a long-term investment with FV = PV × (1+r)^n and answer “how long to double?” on the spot with the rule of 72.
  • You have seen the simple-vs-compound gap: at ¥100,000, 5%, 30 years, compounding finishes about ¥182,000 ahead.
  • You can use “real return ≈ nominal − inflation” to see through “safe” products that cannot outrun inflation.
  • Remember the three ages: start dollar-cost averaging ten years earlier, and the ending value more than doubles.
