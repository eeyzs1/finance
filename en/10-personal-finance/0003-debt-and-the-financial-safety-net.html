Personal Finance · Lesson 3

Debt and the Financial Safety Net

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2

Investing is offense; paying down debt and building safety nets is defense. No prudent investment reliably outruns credit-card debt at 18% a year — repair the balance sheet first, then talk about compounding. This lesson is the construction blueprint for a financial safety net, running from high-interest debt through the emergency fund to insurance and your credit record.

1. The Interest-Rate Spectrum of Debt

Borrow the same ¥10,000, and the interest owed a year later can differ several-fold across debt types. First figure out where on the spectrum each of your debts sits:

Debt typeTypical annual rateTerm / collateralOne-line comment
Home mortgageAbout 3%–5%Up to 30 years / property collateralThe lowest rate and the longest term
Auto loanAbout 4%–8%1–5 years / vehicle collateralA car purchase is consumption — stay within your means
Consumer loans / credit-card installmentsAbout 8%–18%1–3 years / unsecuredThe quoted fee often understates the true annualized rate
Online / small cash loansOften higherMonths to 1 year / unsecuredCheck the effective annual rate; use with care

The ranges above were typical at the time of writing and serve only as teaching reference points; your own loan contract and regulatory disclosures govern. Note also: a credit-card installment plan quoting “0.6% fee per period” usually works out to a true annualized rate above 13%.

Most consumer loans are repaid by amortization (equal installments): the monthly payment is fixed, with interest dominating early on and principal later — which also means the earlier you prepay, the more interest you save.

Good debt or bad debt: rate and use

Two tests: ① how high is the rate; ② where did the money go. A 3.8% mortgage that finances a home you live in, with a comfortable cushion for the monthly payment, can be rational “good debt”; an 18% credit-card installment buying something that depreciates fast is almost certainly “bad debt.” Rule of thumb: any debt whose rate exceeds the expected return of your prudent portfolio (most people estimate 5%–8%) should be cleared first.

2. Avalanche vs. Snowball: Which Debt First?

Suppose you are carrying two debts at once, with a fixed monthly amount for repayment. Which debt does the spare cash attack first? Two classic answers:

Debt avalanche method: pay off the highest-rate debt first. Mathematically it minimizes total interest — the “theoretically optimal” answer.
Debt snowball method: pay off the smallest-balance debt first. It wipes out a whole debt soonest, and the “one more gone” sense of achievement keeps the discipline alive.

Mathematically optimal is not the same as behaviorally optimal: if your self-discipline is strong, use the avalanche and not a cent of extra interest is paid; if you tend to give up halfway, treat the snowball’s extra interest as tuition for the psychology of sticking with it. The shared bottom line of both methods: take on no new debt while repaying. Use the simulator below to see how much the two orders differ at your own monthly payment.

Current focus: the avalanche — attack Debt A at 18% (¥80,000) first; pay only the interest on Debt B each month (about ¥125); once A hits zero, throw everything at B. Mathematically, total interest is the least.

Example debts: A — balance ¥80,000, 18% a year; B — balance ¥30,000, 5% a year. Model simplifications: interest compounds monthly; the non-target debt pays only that month's interest each month (a minimum payment ≈ interest), and everything else goes to the target debt; prepayment penalties and differences in minimum-payment rules are not included.

Verify by hand (default ¥3,000 per month): the first month’s interest = 80000 × 18% ÷ 12 + 30000 × 5% ÷ 12 = 1200 + 125 = ¥1,325; under the avalanche, about ¥1,675 that month genuinely cuts principal. At the defaults the avalanche pays off in about 47 months with roughly ¥29,600 total interest; the snowball takes about 52 months with roughly ¥44,900 — the avalanche pays about ¥15,000 less in interest and lands ashore about 5 months sooner (exact figures follow the simulator’s live output).

3. The Emergency Fund: Before Any Investing

An emergency fund is 3–6 months of the household’s essential expenses, kept in a money market fund or a demand deposit — the point is not the return but “withdrawable anytime, with almost no loss.”

Why 3–6 months? It covers the two most common shocks: the window of finding a new job after a layoff, and a medical event that must be paid up front. The more rigid your expenses (mortgage, children) and the less stable your income (freelance, sales), the closer you should sit to 6 months or more.

Why before any investing? Without an emergency fund, one accident forces you to sell at the market’s low or borrow at 18% a year — destroying compounding and composure at the same time. It amounts to running a stress test on the household finances first: work out what carries you through the worst case, and only then decide how much investment risk you can take.

4. Insurance and Your Credit Record: The Last Two Lines of Defense

Insurance: four basic protections — life, critical illness, medical, and accident — use a small, certain premium to transfer the large risks that could punch through years of savings, forming the household’s protective net; see the Insurance & Actuarial Science branch for details.

Credit record: missed payments on loans and credit cards are reported to the People's Bank of China credit-reference system and directly affect the rates and limits on your future mortgage or auto loan. Paying on time and not overextending your credit keeps your record clean — itself the most overlooked form of “saving money.”

The build order for the safety net

First keep a small pool of ready cash to prevent “borrowing to service debt”; then concentrate firepower on clearing high-interest debt; then fill the 3–6 month emergency fund and basic protections — only once all of these are in place does long-term investing truly begin.

✓ Key takeaways
  • You can rank your debts from lowest to highest rate and spot at a glance the high-interest debt to kill first
  • You can tell the debt avalanche from the debt snowball and choose a payoff order that matches your follow-through
  • You remember: emergency fund = 3–6 months of essential expenses, built before any investing
  • You understand how the insurance safety net and your credit record together form the financial safety net
