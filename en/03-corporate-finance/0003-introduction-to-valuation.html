Introduction to Valuation

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2 on capital structure

What is a company actually worth? Valuation is not guessing tomorrow’s share price; it is converting two questions — how much can it earn, and how much risk must be borne — into a single number you would pay today. This lesson hands you two rulers: relative valuation, which compares a company with others, and absolute valuation, which works it out from the company itself — and lets you see with your own eyes how one nudge of an assumption moves the whole valuation.

1. Relative valuation: P/E — the “years to earn it back” intuition

The most widely used relative-valuation yardstick is the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E): P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share (EPS). The intuition is easy — at today’s earnings level, how many years to “earn your money back”? A 20× P/E is roughly a “20-year payback” (ignoring growth).

Example: a company has EPS of ¥2 and a reasonable industry P/E of 20×, so a fair share price is about 20 × 2 = ¥40. Check: 40 ÷ 2 = 20× — holds. Conversely, if the current price is ¥60, the P/E is 30×, clearly expensive for the industry, unless you have reason to believe the company will grow faster than its peers.

Three ways P/E distorts

① A loss-making company has no P/E — with a negative denominator, the metric simply breaks; ② a low P/E at the top of a cycle is “fake cheapness” — at the peak, earnings are abnormally high, which inflates the denominator and makes the P/E look low; once earnings fall back, the “bargain” shows its true colors; ③ high-growth companies deserve high P/Es — growth sharply shortens the true payback period, and slapping a 20× on a fast grower often condemns it unfairly.

Price-to-book ratio (P/B): how much you pay for ¥1 of book assets

Price-to-book ratio (P/B) = share price ÷ book value per share. It suits asset-heavy industries such as banking, steel, and power — there, the assets are the business. But asset-light companies (brands, software, patents) earn far beyond their book net assets, so their P/B runs high year after year, and comparing through P/B becomes distorted.

EV/EBITDA: smoothing out capital structure and depreciation policy

EV/EBITDA = enterprise value (market cap + net debt) ÷ earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation, and amortization. The numerator counts debt in; the denominator strips interest, taxes, and depreciation out — smoothing away differences in capital structure and accounting policy as far as possible. Fairer than P/E when comparing across companies and countries, it is the darling of M&A and investment banking.

2. Absolute valuation: DCF and the Gordon growth model

Relative valuation compares with others; absolute valuation works from the company itself. Discounted cash flow (DCF) holds that a company’s value = the sum of all its future free cash flows discounted to today — exactly the Lesson 1 NPV idea draped over an entire company. For companies whose dividends grow at a steady rate, discounting to infinity condenses into one elegant formula — the Gordon growth model:

V = D₁ ÷ (r − g)
D₁ = next year’s dividend; r = the discount rate (the return shareholders require, often benchmarked on Lesson 2’s WACC); g = the perpetual dividend growth rate; the formula requires r to stay above g at all times.

Example: D₁ = ¥5, r = 10%, g = 4%: V = 5 ÷ (0.10 − 0.04) = 5 ÷ 0.06 ≈ ¥83.3.

The tug-of-war between r and g is the real protagonist: raise g from 4% to 5% and the denominator shrinks from 0.06 to 0.05, so V jumps from 83.3 to 5 ÷ 0.05 = ¥100 — the assumption moved a single percentage point, and the valuation rose 20% (check: 100 ÷ 83.3 = 1.2).

Nudge an assumption, move the whole valuation

The mathematics of DCF is always correct; the problems always live in the assumptions. g and r are both estimates, the denominator r − g is so small, and the valuation is extremely sensitive to both. The next time you see a “target price of ¥100”, ask first: what assumptions was it fed?

3. Why the “story” matters

You may have noticed: neither g nor r is “looked up” in a market-data terminal; both are inferred from the company’s story — product penetration, pricing power, and competitive moats set g; the interest-rate environment, business risk, and shareholder patience set r. Change the story, and the assumptions change, and the valuation turns upside down. That is why the same company’s “fair price” can differ twofold between a bull market and a bear market: what changed is not just the price, but the narrative.

A healthy workflow

Use the model to organize the logic, use the story to test the assumptions, and use the market price to test both. When the three quarrel, that is not a disaster — it is where research begins.

✓ Key takeaways
  • The P/E “years to earn it back” intuition, the 20 × 2 = ¥40 example, and the three distortion cases
  • P/B fits asset-heavy industries; EV/EBITDA smooths out capital-structure and depreciation differences
  • The DCF idea and the Gordon model V = D₁ ÷ (r − g), with the 5 ÷ 0.06 ≈ ¥83.3 check
  • Sensitivity intuition: g moves one point and the valuation rises 20%; the science of valuation lies in the formulas, the art lies in the assumptions
