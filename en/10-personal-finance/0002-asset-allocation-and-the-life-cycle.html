Asset Allocation and the Life Cycle

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1

The last lesson watched the long-run tug-of-war between compounding and inflation; this one answers a more fundamental question — where should the money actually go? How much sits in stocks, bonds, and cash shapes long-term wealth far more than the “which fund, and when” questions most people pour their effort into.

1. Allocation Deserves Your Effort More Than Timing

In 1986, Brinson, Hood, and Beebower analyzed the performance of 91 large US pension funds (a classic study cited again and again in the later literature) and concluded that most of the return differences between portfolios could be explained by asset allocation policy — that is, how much sat in stocks, bonds, and cash — while security selection and timing contributed far less. Later research has argued plenty about the exact percentage, but the direction has long been industry consensus.

The implication for ordinary investors is direct: asset allocation is strategy; product selection and timing are only tactics. Homework spent on the “mix” pays far better than homework spent on guessing next week’s moves.

Set the mix first, then shop for products

Within the same market, getting the stock-bond mix wrong usually costs far more than picking the wrong fund. The mix decides which boat you sit in; product selection only decides which seat you take.

2. Three Asset Classes, Each with Its Own Job

There are hundreds of investable asset classes, but the skeleton of a portfolio is usually just three:

Asset classLong-run returnVolatilityRole in the portfolio
Stocks (equity funds)Highest over the long runLargestGrowth engine; its job is beating inflation over the long run
Bonds (bond funds)MediumMediumStabilizer; low correlation with stocks, often strengthens when panic hits
Cash / money market fundsLowerVery smallLiquidity pool; the emergency reserve and rebalancing ammunition

Exact numbers differ across markets, but the pecking order — stocks: high return, high volatility; bonds: middling; cash: safe — is remarkably stable. Why do low or negative correlations cut portfolio risk? For the mechanism, see the lesson Diversification and Correlation.

3. The Life Cycle: How Age Changes Your Allocation

Count yourself as an asset too: the salary you will earn over the coming decades is a fairly steady stream of cash flows, which finance calls human capital. At 25 that stream still has 35-plus years to run; at 60, only a few. When stocks crash 40%, the young have a long salary stream to keep buying with while they wait for recovery; someone near retirement turns a paper loss into a realized loss with every withdrawal for living expenses.

Hence the broad rule of thumb: the younger you are, the higher your stock share can be; the closer you are to retirement, the more deliberately you should dial down portfolio volatility. But age is not the only variable — your risk tolerance also depends on how steady your income is, how much debt you carry, and whether a halved account would cost you sleep and make you capitulate at the bottom. Of your financial capacity and psychological capacity, plan around whichever is lower.

How to use “100 − age = stock share %”

The maxim gives a quick anchor: age 30 → 70% stocks, age 60 → 40% stocks. It has value, but it is a one-line heuristic, not a law: it knows nothing about your income stability, your debts and family obligations, or whether you could sleep through a 30% drawdown. Treat it as a starting point, then adjust up or down to your own circumstances.

The lazy option: target-date funds

A target-date fund is named for its expected retirement year (say, “Target Date 2050”); as the date approaches it automatically steps down the stock share and rebalances on a schedule — the two jobs of this lesson, packaged and handed to discipline to execute.

4. Rebalancing: “Sell High, Buy Low” Made Automatic

Suppose your target is 60% stocks / 40% bonds. After a big stock-market year the mix drifts to 70/30; rebalancing then means selling some of the stocks that ran up and buying the bonds that lagged, restoring the 60/40 split. Mechanically, it does “sell what rose, buy what fell” — precisely the selling-high-and-buying-low that most people know they should do but cannot.

Common practice: check once a year on a fixed date, or trigger whenever an asset class drifts more than 5 percentage points from target. Rebalancing is, at bottom, rules fighting the instinct to chase winners and dump losers — the same lineage as the “disciplined investing” advice of behavioral finance; see the lesson Bubbles and Market Anomalies.

5. Fees: Compounding’s Invisible Opponent

Lesson 1 showed compounding to be time’s friend; fees are the opposite — each year they shave a little off the return, and the remainder then compounds on a smaller base. Over 10 years the difference hardly shows; over 30 years it is staggering.

1.5% vs. 0.15%: about ¥230,000 apart over 30 years

Invest the same ¥100,000 at a 7% gross annual return, held for 30 years, with fees deducted from returns every year — here is how the two fee levels end up.

Ending value = ¥100,000 × (1 + 7% − annual fee rate)30
The fee is not deducted once; it is deducted every year, and each shortfall then compounds in turn — a tiny difference in fee rates, magnified by 30 years of compounding into a difference in the order of magnitude of wealth.
  • Fee 1.50%: net return = 7% − 1.50% = 5.50%, 1.05530 ≈ 4.984, ending value ≈ ¥100,000 × 4.984 ≈ ¥498,000
  • Fee 0.15%: net return = 7% − 0.15% = 6.85%, 1.068530 ≈ 7.299, ending value ≈ ¥100,000 × 7.299 ≈ ¥730,000
  • The two differ by about ¥231,000; the low-fee portfolio ends roughly 46% ahead — choosing low-fee broad index products is often the most certain “excess return” an ordinary investor can get

✓ Key takeaways
  • You can explain why asset allocation deserves more effort than timing and security selection

  • You understand how human capital sets the benchmark stock weight at each age — and that “100 − age” is only a heuristic
  • You know the mechanism and triggers of rebalancing — disciplined selling high and buying low
