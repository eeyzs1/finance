Capital Structure and Leverage

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 1 on NPV

Running a company takes money, and money has only two sources: borrowed (debt), or put up by the shareholders themselves (equity). That mixing ratio is capital structure. Using a single ¥10 million balance sheet, this lesson shows how debt magnifies shareholders’ returns and their risks at the same time — and when it turns from friend into enemy.

1. Two sources of money: debt and equity

Suppose a company will invest ¥10 million in a new plant. That ¥10 million is either borrowed or put up by shareholders. The two kinds of money have utterly different temperaments:

Debt: creditors with a fixed slice. Bank loans and corporate bonds both belong here. Three key features: ① fixed interest — borrow ¥4 million at 5% and, however bad the year, ¥0.2 million of interest still falls due; ② priority in liquidation — if the company goes bankrupt, creditors line up ahead of shareholders and get paid first; ③ interest is tax-deductible — interest is deducted from pre-tax profit, as though the government ceded part of its tax take; the money saved this way is called the tax shield.

Equity: everything left over is yours. The company pays wages, then interest, then income tax; only what remains goes to shareholders — the so-called “residual claim”. The price is standing last in line for returns; the perks are no maturity date (the principal never has to be repaid) and no ceiling on the upside.

One-line memory hook

Creditors take a “fixed slice”; shareholders take “everything left over”. The ratio in which debt and equity cut this pie is the capital structure — and how you cut it completely changes how violently shareholder returns swing.

2. A leverage worked example: one company, two recipes

Hold the assets fixed, vary the debt ratio, and the swings in shareholder returns are magnified — that is financial leverage. Let the company hold ¥10 million of assets, with earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) moving with the economy: ¥2 million in a boom, ¥1 million in a normal year, ¥0.4 million in a recession. Compare two structures:

Structure A: zero debt; shareholders put up all ¥10 million. Structure B: borrow ¥4 million (a 40% debt ratio, at 5% interest, for annual interest of 400 × 5% = ¥0.2 million); shareholders put up only ¥6 million.

ROE = (EBIT − interest) ÷ shareholders’ equity
To keep the leverage effect in focus, income taxes are simplified away throughout this example; Structure B’s interest = 400 × 5% = ¥0.2 million.

To tell whether leverage is helper or enemy, look at one thing only

Whether the return on assets (ROA = EBIT ÷ 10) exceeds the interest rate. In the boom and normal scenarios ROA is 20% and 10%, both above the 5% rate, so leverage lifts shareholder returns from 20% to 30% and from 10% to 13.3% — borrow at 5% to run a business earning 20%, and the spread belongs to shareholders. In the recession ROA is only 4%, below the 5% rate, and leverage turns on its master: 3.3% now trails the unlevered 4.0%.

How thin is the cushion in a recession?

Structure B owes ¥0.2 million of interest every year; with recession EBIT of ¥0.4 million, only ¥0.2 million of profit is left. The moment EBIT drops below ¥0.2 million, shareholders take nothing or lose money, and the company may fail to cover even its interest — and the costs that follow, the lawyers’ fees, fire-sale disposals, and lost customers and talent, are the financial distress costs. Structure A carries no such death sentence.

3. The MM theorem: slicing the pie does not change its size

Modigliani and Miller’s famous 1958 theorem says: in a perfect market — no taxes, no bankruptcy costs, fully transparent information — a company’s value depends only on how well its assets generate cash flow. How the pie is sliced does not change the total: no matter how much is borrowed, creditors plus shareholders together always receive the same whole pie, and whatever shareholders gain is exactly eaten up by creditors’ compensation for risk.

The real world is imperfect in precisely the opposite way: the tax deductibility of interest (the tax shield) encourages more debt, while financial distress costs warn against borrowing too much. The two forces tug in opposite directions, and out comes a not-too-high, not-too-low optimal capital structure — which is why most mature companies hold their debt ratios in the middle range rather than at 0% or 90%.

How to remember it

The MM theorem gives you the ideal reference frame: slicing does not matter. The tug-of-war between the tax shield and bankruptcy costs gives you the real-world answer: slicing does matter, and there is a middle optimum.

4. WACC: the blended cost of financing

A company both borrows and issues shares — so what is its overall cost of funds? Weight the two by their shares of market value and average them, and you get the weighted average cost of capital (WACC):

WACC = (D÷V) × interest rate on debt × (1 − tax rate) + (E÷V) × cost of equity
D and E are the market values of debt and equity, V = D + E; multiplying the cost of debt by (1 − tax rate) is precisely how the tax shield shows up in the accounting.

Intuitively: debt is cheap — its interest rate is low (there is a tax shield, and priority in liquidation provides a backstop) — but as borrowing piles up, creditors’ risk rises, interest rates climb, and cheap money turns expensive. Equity is dear but resilient — shareholders stand last in line and therefore demand a high return, yet the company never has to repay them principal or interest, so one stumble does not starve it of funds. WACC is the weighted outcome of this trade-off, and it is the discount rate most often used in next lesson’s valuation.

✓ Key takeaways
  • The essential contrast between debt and equity: fixed interest + priority in liquidation + tax shield vs. residual claim + no maturity date
  • Leverage magnifies ROE only while ROA exceeds the interest rate; the point of no return is EBIT falling below interest
  • The MM theorem is the ideal reference frame; in reality the tax shield and financial distress costs tug their way to an optimal capital structure
  • WACC intuition: debt is cheap but dearer the more you use; equity is dear but resilient; the weighted blend is the overall cost
