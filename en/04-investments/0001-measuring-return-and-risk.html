Measuring Return and Risk: the numbers that matter on a fund page

About 15 minutesDifficulty: Advanced beginnerPrerequisites: 02 Money & Banking recommended first

Open the fact sheet of any fund and two sets of numbers always grab the eye: how much it earned per year on average, and how violently its returns bounced around. This lesson untangles both in one go: how an investment’s return is defined and verified, how “possible futures” are weighted into an expectation, how the size of the bounces becomes a comparable risk metric, and why “earning 8% a year on average” often overstates what you actually pocket. Every key number is checked on the spot.

1. How much did you actually make: an investment’s bookkeeping

Every discussion starts from one concept — rate of return. It converts “how much money did I make” into “how much did each ¥1 of principal make,” which is what puts a ¥10,000 fund and a ¥300 stock on the same table for comparison. The most basic version is the holding-period return:

r = (ending price P₁ − beginning price P₀ + dividends D received over the period) ÷ beginning price P₀
The numerator is the total payoff you actually take in over the holding period (price change + dividends); the denominator is the principal you put in. Multiply the result by 100% to state it as a percentage.

Example: buy a stock for ¥10 at the start of the year, collect a cash dividend of ¥0.5 while holding it, and sell it for ¥11 at year-end:

r = (11 − 10 + 0.5) ÷ 10 = 1.5 ÷ 10 = 15%
Check: the price change earns 11 − 10 = ¥1 and the dividend adds another ¥0.5, for a total of ¥1.5; divide by the ¥10 principal to get 15%.

Two traps to avoid: first, dividends must be counted — looking only at the price change understates the true return; second, 15% is the return over that particular holding period — 15% over one month and 15% over one year are entirely different things, so before comparing across assets, first convert everything to the same horizon (for example, annualized).

2. Weighting the “possible futures” into an average

The 15% is after-the-fact bookkeeping, but investment decisions are made before the fact — the future hasn’t happened yet, and all you hold is a list of possible scenarios. Weight each scenario’s rate of return by its probability of occurring and average them, and you get the expected return, usually written μ:

Expected return μ = Σ probabilityᵢ × returnᵢ
Intuition: multiply each outcome by its chance of occurring, then add them up. It answers “what do I get on average,” not “what will I get this time.”

Suppose a stock’s performance next year has only three possibilities:

Market stateProbabilityReturn in that stateContribution to the expectation = probability × return
Bull market30%+20%0.3 × 20 = +6.0 percentage points
Normal50%+5%0.5 × 5 = +2.5 percentage points
Bear market20%−10%0.2 × (−10) = −2.0 percentage points
Expected return μTotal 100%—+6.5%

Check: 0.3 × 20 + 0.5 × 5 + 0.2 × (−10) = 6.0 + 2.5 − 2.0 = +6.5%; the three probabilities 30% + 50% + 20% = 100%, so the scenario list is complete.

The expectation is not a promise

μ = +6.5% does not correspond to any actual scenario — none of the three possibilities is “up 6.5%.” Nor is it the most likely outcome: in the example above, the most likely outcome is actually +5% (probability 50%). The expectation is only the “average level” you use for decisions; real results bounce around it.

3. Putting a number on uncertainty

“Bouncing around” is itself the risk. Imagine two stocks, both with an expected return of 6.5%: one lands between 5% and 8% year after year, while the other either doubles or halves — that difference demands a number of its own. Finance defines risk as “the uncertainty of outcomes” and measures it with volatility (standard deviation), written σ:

σ = √[ Σ probabilityᵢ × (returnᵢ − μ)² ]
Intuition, no derivation: see how far each outcome sits from the expectation, square that distance (removing the sign and heavily penalizing extremes), weight by probability and sum, then take the square root to return to the original units. σ answers “how far do I typically stray from the expectation?”

Verify it with the Section 2 example (returns in percentage points):

Check: 54.675 + 1.125 + 54.45 = 110.25, and 10.5² = 110.25, so σ = √110.25 = 10.5%. What it means: in a “typical year” this stock deviates from the 6.5% expectation by about 10.5 percentage points — counting both directions. The larger σ is, the more spread out the possible outcomes and the greater the risk; σ = 0 would mean the outcome is completely certain.

4. The normal approximation and the 68/95 rule

A lone σ still isn’t intuitive — it needs a “translation rule.” Finance commonly approximates the distribution of annual returns with the normal distribution (the bell curve), and then μ and σ convert directly into ranges: about 68% of years fall within μ ± 1σ, and about 95% within μ ± 2σ. This is the 68/95 rule.

μ ± 1σ: 6.5% ± 10.5% = [−4.0%, +17.0%], about 68% of years
μ ± 2σ: 6.5% ± 21.0% = [−14.5%, +27.5%], about 95% of years
Check: 6.5 − 10.5 = −4.0; 6.5 + 10.5 = 17.0; 6.5 − 21.0 = −14.5; 6.5 + 21.0 = 27.5. Read the other way: about 16% of years fall below −4% (roughly once every 6 years), and about 2.5% fall below −14.5% (about once every 40 years).
The limits of this approximation

Real market return distributions are often “fat-tailed” — extreme booms and crashes happen more often than the normal curve predicts (2008 is a famous example). The 68/95 rule is a yardstick for quick estimates, not an insurance contract; the larger σ is, the bigger the discount you should apply to this yardstick.

② The smaller σ, the thinner the red region. Drag the slider to 5%: z = −1.6 and the loss probability drops to about 5.8%; drag it to 30%: z ≈ −0.27 and the loss probability climbs to about 40%. The expected return μ never moved, yet “will it lose money” changed beyond recognition — which is why quoting a return must always come with σ.

③ A higher μ does not mean you are more likely to make money. B’s expectation is only 2 percentage points above A’s, yet at the default settings its loss probability is more than ten times A’s. How much uncertainty you will trade for how much expected return is the core question investments keeps answering — and diversification, the subject of the next lesson, is the first tool that can push σ down while sacrificing little μ.

6. Volatility drag: the arithmetic mean can fool you

One last numbers game, one that countless investors have been burned by: taking the arithmetic average of each year’s returns systematically overstates your true annualized return. The true annualized figure must be computed by compounding, that is, with the geometric mean:

True annualized return = [(1+r₁) × (1+r₂) × … × (1+rₙ)]^(1/n) − 1
First multiply the “wealth multipliers” of the years together (up 30% means multiplying by 1.30, down 14% by 0.86), then take the n-th root, and finally subtract 1.
Key result: arithmetic mean ≠ actual annualized return

Year 1 +30%, year 2 −14%: the arithmetic mean = (30% − 14%) ÷ 2 = 8%, which looks lovely; but two-year total wealth = 1.30 × 0.86 = 1.118, a gain of only 11.8% in all, so the true annualized return = 1.1180.5 − 1 ≈ 5.7% (check: 1.0574² ≈ 1.118). Gain 30% first, lose 14% after — more than 2 percentage points of that “8% a year on average” were an illusion. Volatility itself eats returns: this is volatility drag, and the bigger the volatility, the heavier the drag.

Even more sobering is the asymmetry of losses: reverse the order — down 30% first, then up 14% — and total wealth = 0.70 × 1.14 = 0.798, still a 20.2% two-year loss, without the principal even recovered. Losing 30% of principal requires a further gain of 1 ÷ 0.70 − 1 ≈ 42.9% just to break even. The deeper the fall, the harder the recovery — the other face of volatility drag, and the reason “controlling σ is itself protecting returns.”

✓ Key takeaways
  • Compute a holding-period return: r = (ending price − beginning price + dividends) ÷ beginning price; and remember that dividends must be counted and cross-period returns must first be annualized.
  • Compute an expected return μ by probability weighting (three-state example: +6.5%), and remember that the expectation is not a promise.
  • Measure the “typical deviation from the expectation” with the standard deviation σ (same example: 10.5%) and convert it into return ranges with the 68/95 rule.
  • Know that the true annualized return is the geometric mean: the arithmetic mean overstates it (8% vs 5.7% in the example), and the greater the volatility, the larger the overstatement.
