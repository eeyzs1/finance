Behavioral Finance · Lesson 3

Bubbles and Market Anomalies

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: Lesson 2

In Lesson 2 the biases lived inside a single head — your own; this lesson scales them up to the whole market — when the biases of millions of people tilt in the same direction, you get herds and bubbles. We will dissect the five stages of a bubble’s life, meet a few “market anomalies,” and finally put together a personal anti-bubble kit. This is also the final lesson of the Behavioral Finance branch.

1. Herding: Following the Crowd Isn’t Pure Folly

Herding is often used as a term of mockery, but it has a rational component: other people’s trades carry information in themselves — they may have studied something you haven’t, so “following along” is sometimes the smart, effort-saving move. The danger is the informational cascade: once you discard your own information and simply imitate the people ahead of you, the people behind you will imitate you in turn. Once a cascade has formed, no new information enters the price — everyone’s “consensus” actually rests on the first few accidental moves.

The cascade’s fuel is FOMO (the fear of missing out). The pain of watching others make money registers on the value function much like a real loss — the mirror image of Lesson 1’s loss aversion: what is lost is not just “money not made” but “money that could have been made.” So the more prices rise, the more people buy; the more people buy, the more prices rise.

2. The Life of a Bubble: Kindleberger’s Five Stages

The economic historian Charles Kindleberger, in Manias, Panics, and Crashes, distilled the life of a bubble into five stages:

StageWhat happens in the marketThe novice at this point
① StealthA new narrative appears; only a few notice, skeptics dominate, prices creep up“I don’t get it” — completely indifferent
② ExpansionSmart money enters, the media starts to follow, price gains accelerateAware of it, but says “I’ll buy the pullback”
③ EuphoriaLeverage and new players flood in, valuation common sense stops working, “this time is different” is loudestAfraid of missing out (FOMO), goes all-in chasing the top
④ Profit-takingInsiders quietly exit, prices turn down, liquidity starts to dry upTreats the decline as a pullback, buys more on every fall
⑤ CrashSellers can’t find buyers, liquidity vanishes, a stampede is followed by a long bottomingDeeply underwater: “I’ll sell the moment I break even”

Three historical footnotes, one sentence each: the Dutch tulip mania of 1637 — rare bulbs at one point traded for the price of a mansion on the canal, and after the crash countless contracts were voided; the NASDAQ dot-com bubble of 2000 — the index raced from about 2200 in early 1999 to 5048 in March 2000, then lost about 78% over the following two-plus years; crypto assets in 2021 — loose global liquidity pushed Bitcoin to an all-time high of about $69,000, and it drew down more than 70% within a year.

3. Market Anomalies: The Regularities Hiding in Prices

If bubbles are the extreme eruption of biases, market anomalies are the everyday fingerprints biases leave behind. The two most famous anomalies are neatly complementary in time scale:

Momentum: Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) found that a “winner portfolio” selected on the past 3–12 months of performance kept beating the “loser portfolio” on average over the following 3–12 months — the strong stay strong, and trends exist in the short-to-medium term. On the 3–5 year horizon, De Bondt and Thaler (1985) found a long-term reversal: the portfolio of the past few years’ most miserable stocks went on to lead on average — extreme performance eventually undergoes mean reversion. There are also calendar effects (seasonal patterns such as the “January effect”); they are small, come and go, and many weakened markedly once the papers were published.

Why haven’t these anomalies been arbitraged away?

Because arbitrage is no free lunch: there are transaction costs and leverage constraints, and your clients or your boss will tolerate your being “wrong” for only a few months, while an anomaly can stay “overdone” for years — these are the limits of arbitrage (Shleifer & Vishny, 1997). As Keynes’s famous line is paraphrased: “Markets can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent.”

4. The Five-Piece Anti-Bubble Kit

You cannot predict when the next bubble will begin, but you can write your rules for dealing with any bubble in advance. The most effective vehicle is an investment policy statement (IPS) — treat it as a contract between you and the future “FOMO-ing you.”

The five-piece anti-bubble kit (write it into your investment policy statement)

① Write an investment policy statement that fixes objectives, position sizes, and discipline in advance; ② automate dollar-cost averaging so “when to buy” is set by the calendar, not by mood; ③ rebalance on a schedule (say, once a year) to force yourself to sell high and buy low; ④ cap any single position (for example, no more than 10% of total assets); ⑤ impose a 48-hour cooling-off period on major decisions — sleep on it before placing the order.

✓ Key takeaways
  • Recite the five bubble stages in order and match historical cases to them
  • Understand herding and informational cascades: following the crowd has a rational core, but once a cascade forms, information stops updating
  • Distinguish the time scales of momentum (3–12 months) and long-term reversal (3–5 years)
  • Explain why arbitrage cannot eliminate anomalies (limits of arbitrage)
  • Use the five-piece anti-bubble kit: policy statement, dollar-cost averaging, rebalancing, position caps, a 48-hour cooling-off period
