The Evolution of Payments and Clearing: What Happens the Moment You Scan

About 15 minutesDifficulty: BeginnerPrerequisites: 02 Money & Banking recommended

Your phone beeps and the checkout screen says payment successful — yet in that instant, no money has flown from your account into the merchant’s pocket. What finishes within seconds is a relay of authorization messages; the actual transfer of funds waits for end-of-day clearing. This lesson takes that invisible chain apart, link by link, then contrasts it with the e-CNY, which lets “money” fly peer-to-peer again.

1. Four Steps Up: How Payments Got to Today

Compress payment history into four steps and each one answers the same question: how can two people who never meet and don’t know each other hand over money safely?

StepHow you payInformation flow vs. funds flowWhat changed
CashMoney and goods change hands togetherUnited: the deal is done when the money arrivesInstant and anonymous, but requires both parties face to face
Bank cardThe card is authorized; the bank moves the moneySeparation begins: authorize first, clear laterA clearing intermediary enters; for the first time a transaction can happen without meeting
Mobile payment / QR codeScan; done in one stepStill separate: one step up front, while the back-end chain is no shorterAuthorization in seconds and a minimalist experience — the chain is hidden inside the phone
Central bank digital currencyThe e-CNY moves peer-to-peerConverging again: value moves together with the transactionManaged anonymity, dual offline payment, and positioning as an M0 replacement (see 02 Money & Banking)

Note the third step in particular: the QR code reduces the act of “paying” to a single step, but it does not shorten the clearing chain — it only hides the chain inside the experience. The fourth step is the one that truly tries to rewrite the chain.

2. The Four-Party Model: The Invisible Relay Behind a QR Payment

A bank-card or QR transaction typically has four parties present: the issuing bank on the payer’s side, the acquirer on the merchant’s side, the card network switching in the middle, and the merchant that initiates the charge. This division of labor is the so-called four-party model.

RoleWhich sideWhat it doesWhere it earns fees
Issuing bankPayer’s sideHolds the payer’s account: verifies balance and risk controls, keeps the booksOne of the main sharers of the merchant fee
AcquirerMerchant’s sideProvides the merchant’s QR code and acceptance services, passes transactions to the clearing organizationCharges the merchant an acceptance fee, then shares it by rule
Card network (clearing)Central hubSwitches messages, sets the rules, reconciles totals and nets positionsCollects network service fees under its clearing rules
MerchantReceiving sideInitiates the charge; displays the goods and the QR codeUltimately bears the acceptance fee

Exact rates at each link vary with the market and with regulation; this lesson memorizes mechanisms, not numbers.

The key picture: information flow and funds flow are separate

Scan ¥100 and the two flows sit apart in time. The information flow completes in seconds: merchant → acquirer → NetsUnion/UnionPay → issuing bank for verification, then an authorization receipt returns along the same path; the funds flow waits for end of day — only after the clearing organization nets positions does money actually move, through settlement, from the payer’s bank to the merchant’s bank. “Payment successful” refers to the former; “money received” refers to the latter.

A participant’s net receivable = Σ (receivables) − Σ (payables)
The clearing organization does not move money trade by trade; it nets each participant’s transactions for the day into a single amount and transfers it in one sweep. This is one reason the funds flow waits for end of day: only when all the accounts are in can the netting be worked out.

3. Cutting the Direct Connections: Bringing the Funds Flow Back into Daylight

After mobile payments exploded, third-party payment providers were “directly connected” with each bank: a provider opened accounts at every bank and scattered its customer reserve funds across them, while transactions ran over the provider’s own rails. The result — no single bank could piece together the full picture of the funds, and the scattered reserves left room for misappropriation.

The reform came in two steps. First, “cutting the direct connections”: network payment transactions that touch bank accounts must all switch through NetsUnion Clearing (or UnionPay), and providers may no longer connect with banks directly. Second, reserve funds moved from scattered holdings to centralized deposit with the People’s Bank of China.

Remember in one sentence

NetsUnion’s direct-connection cutoff plus centralized custody of reserve funds is, in essence, about bringing the funds flow of third-party payments back, whole and visible, inside the clearing system and the regulator’s field of view.

4. The e-CNY: Making Money Peer-to-Peer Again

The e-CNY (digital renminbi) is issued by the People’s Bank of China and is a central bank liability — in plain words, it is “cash in digital form”. It differs in essence from a third-party payment balance: a balance is a liability of the payment provider or bank to you (deposit-like), while the e-CNY is the central bank’s direct promise to you. On the monetary aggregates it corresponds to M0 (currency in circulation), echoing the M0 concept in 02 Money & Banking.

DimensionCashThird-party payment balancee-CNY
What it isPaper money issued by the central bankA liability of the payment provider or bankDigital cash issued by the central bank (an M0 replacement)
AnonymityFully anonymousReal-name account systemManaged anonymity: small amounts protect privacy; large amounts are traceable by law

One-sentence comparison: QR payments optimize the “experience”, while the e-CNY rewrites “the form of money” — the former hides the four-party relay inside your phone; the latter lets value itself fly peer-to-peer.
